What Stage 1 computing covers

Computational ThinkingAlgorithms for everyday tasks: following the steps, putting them in order, spotting the one step that is wrong, giving directions with forward, backwards, left and right, and changing the steps to change what happens.
ProgrammingTurning an algorithm into a program a computer can run; predicting what it will do; running it to test it; finding out why it went wrong; and debugging it.
Managing DataAsking a computer the right way for different kinds of question; recording answers with a form; reading a data table to answer a question; letting a computer sort and organise.
Networks and Digital CommunicationDevices joined into a network, with wires and without; the internet as many computers joined around the world; and the times when the internet is not there.
Computer SystemsWhat computers are for; one computer running many programs; inputs and outputs; the computers hidden inside everyday things; what a robot is and where robots work.

Do it unplugged, for real

  • Follow the algorithm: get dressed. Write the steps on cards, one per card. The child reads them out and the grown-up does EXACTLY what each card says, silly mistakes and all - a computer follows its algorithm the same way.
  • Toast algorithm. You need: A grown-up, a kitchen, some bread. Tell your grown-up every step for making toast, one at a time. Your grown-up does EXACTLY what you say, and nothing you did not say. If you forget a step, watch what happens. Look for: Did you say 'take the bread out of the bag'? A computer would not know.
  • Robot grown-up. You need: A grown-up and a pair of shoes. Your grown-up is a robot. It only does what you say. Give the steps for putting on shoes. Say the steps in a different order and see what the robot does. Look for: The order matters. Shoes before socks looks very silly.
  • Draw an algorithm. You need: Paper and crayons. Pick a job: making a sandwich, brushing teeth, feeding a pet. Draw one picture for each step, in a row. Number the pictures 1, 2, 3, 4. Look for: Could somebody follow your pictures without asking you anything?

    3. In the tower algorithm we add 'put another brick on' before the flag. What happens? → the tower is taller

Do it unplugged, for real

  • Drive Robo to the flower. Make a grid on the floor with tape or paper squares. One person is Robo and only does Forward, Backwards, Turn left and Turn right; the other gives the instructions to reach a toy.
  • Blindfold robot. You need: A grown-up, a scarf, a safe room with a toy on the floor. The grown-up is the robot and wears the scarf over their eyes. You give ONLY these instructions: forward, backwards, turn left, turn right. Guide the robot to the toy. Look for: What happens when you say turn left and the robot faces you? Their left is not your left.
  • Floor grid. You need: Masking tape or sheets of paper, a toy. Make a grid of squares on the floor, four by four. Put a toy on one square. Stand on another. A grown-up gives you a program. Do exactly what it says. Did you reach the toy? Look for: One square per forward. A turn spins you but you stay on your square.
  • Treasure path. You need: Paper, a pencil, a sticker. Draw a grid of squares and put a sticker on one square: the treasure. Draw Robo on another square with an arrow for which way it faces. Write the program: F for forward, L and R for the turns. Look for: Give it to a grown-up. Can they follow it to the treasure without asking you anything?

    4. Robo bumps into a wall. What happens? → it stops; the program stops

    6. Before you press Go, a good programmer... → predicts where Robo will stop
    7. Robo faces up. It does 'backwards'. Which way does it move? → down, one square

Do it unplugged, for real

  • Run it, find the bug, fix it. Write a four-card program with one wrong card. Act it out, spot which card went wrong, swap it, and act it out again.
  • Test before you trust. Draw the blocks on paper cards (move right, jump, say hello). The child lays out a program; the grown-up is the computer and acts it out block by block.
  • Bugs in everyday algorithms. Say the steps of a job in the wrong order, or with one silly step in it, and ask the child to spot the bug and say what it should be.
  • Debug a grown-up. You need: Paper cards with instructions, a grown-up. Write a four-card program for making a drink, with ONE wrong card in it.   • Test it. You need: A toy car or a ball, some tape. Write a program to get the car from the door to the table: forward 3, turn right, forward 2. Run it by pushing the car one step per instruction. Did it arrive? If not, find which instruction is wrong. Look for: A program you have not run is a program you have not tested.

    7. Where did the word bug come from? → a real insect found inside an old computer
    8. A program did exactly what we wanted. Does it have a bug? → no, it works
