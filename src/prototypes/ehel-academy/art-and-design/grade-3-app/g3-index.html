Ehel Academy · Art & Design

Grade 3 Art & Design

Eight lessons, in the order they are meant to be done. Every one is about making and looking: paints to mix, marks to make, patterns to build, art from far away to look at, and a journal to keep. About 330 minutes in all, one lesson a week in two short sittings - and a screen cannot be paint, so every lesson sends you to make something real at home.

What Stage 3 Art & Design covers

ExperiencingGathering patterns from buildings in a sketchbook; trying pencils from hard to soft and charcoal; looking closely at a brick carved long ago, a stitched quilt, a painted house wall, a swirling night sky and a great wave; and comparing how different artists used their materials.
MakingShowing light and shade with graded pencils; mixing across the colour wheel; building a clay relief; stitching, weaving or fusing a textile; and planning a mural with a mock-up before it is made big.
ReflectingChecking our work against a goal; giving praise with one useful next step; saying how another artist's work helped us; and changing a colour scheme or a size to make work better.
Thinking and Working ArtisticallyTurning a sketch into a design; changing a mural after thinking about who will see it; borrowing a technique from an artist; and showing that one idea can be made in more than one way.

Make it for real

  • Patterns on a building. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • How will you record it?. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • Which kind of pattern?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • From building to print design. Make a pattern with things from the kitchen: spoon, fork, spoon, fork. Ask what comes next. Then let the child make one for you to continue.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Pattern walk. You need: A small notebook, a pencil, a crayon and a grown-up. Walk down your street with a grown-up. Sketch three patterns you find on buildings: bricks, railings, tiles or windows. Make a rubbing of one bumpy surface. Write one annotation beside each sketch. Look for: Which pattern could become a print?
  • A sketchbook page. You need: A page of your sketchbook, pencils and paints. Choose one pattern you gathered. Draw it three different ways: bigger, smaller, and in new colours. Circle the one you like best and write why. Look for: What did you change each time?
  • Foam print. You need: A foam printing block or a flat polystyrene tray, a blunt pencil, paint, a roller or brush, and a grown-up. Draw your pattern on the foam, pressing the lines in. Roll or brush paint over the foam. Press paper on top, rub the back, and peel it off. Look for: Did the pressed lines stay white?

Make it for real

  • Pencil tones, light to dark. Mix a little black or white into one colour, four times, and paint four patches. Cut them out and put them in order from light to dark.
  • Which pencil for the job?. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • Light and shadow in a drawing. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • Light, shade or cast shadow?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Light and dark lines. Give the child a pencil, a brush, a sponge and a piece of chalk. Ask for a straight line, a wavy line, a zigzag and lots of dots with each one. Which tool made the thickest mark?
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Pencil ladder. You need: Two or three different pencils (like 2H, HB and 4B) and paper. Draw a row of small boxes. Shade the first with the hardest pencil, gently. Shade the next ones with softer pencils. Write the letter under each box. Look for: Which pencil gave the darkest box?
  • Lamp and apple. You need: An apple or a ball, a lamp or a sunny window, a pencil and paper, and a grown-up. Put the apple on a table with the light on one side. Draw its round edge. Shade the side away from the light, and draw the cast shadow on the table. Leave a white spot for the highlight. Look for: Does your apple look round?
  • Charcoal night. You need: A stick of charcoal, an eraser, paper, and old clothes. Cover the paper with charcoal, gently, using the side of the stick. Use the eraser to lift out a moon and some stars. Wash your hands when you finish. Look for: Can you make the moon glow by rubbing out more?

Answer keys

  • Pencil tones, light to dark light to dark, by the colours' own lightness: 2H pencil → H pencil → HB pencil → 2B pencil → 6B pencil
  • Which pencil for the job?
    • light guide lines you can rub out later (needs light) → A 2H pencil
    • the darkest shadow under an apple (needs dark) → A 6B pencil, Charcoal
    • a big, dark stormy sky, fast (needs big) → Charcoal
    • the white shine on a shiny apple (needs highlight) → An eraser
    • an even middle grey (needs middle) → An HB pencil
  • Light and shadow in a drawing find: The light comes from the top left. Everything else in the drawing follows from that.; The brightest spot, facing the light. The artist left the paper white here.; The side facing away from the light is dark. The artist used a soft pencil and short lines here.; The apple blocks the light, so a dark shadow falls on the table, away from the light.. Where is the light coming from? → the top left
  • Light, shade or cast shadow? In the light: the side of a ball facing the lamp, the shiny spot on a cup, facing the lamp; In shade: the side of the ball facing away from the lamp, the back of a box, away from the lamp; Cast shadow: the dark shape on the table beside the ball, the dark shape of a tree lying on the grass, your shadow on the playground
  • Light and dark lines the page judges the stroke itself: a pale guide line with the pencil; a dark charcoal line with the charcoal; the round edge of an apple with the pencil; a long cast shadow with the charcoal. Then a free drawing: no key
  • Tone spotter
    1. What does the H on a pencil mean? → hard
    2. Which is darker? → a 6B pencil
    3. The shadow a ball throws on the table is… → a cast shadow
    4. Which tool makes a bright shine? → an eraser
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: put 5 colours in order from light to dark; chose A 2H pencil for light guide lines you can rub out later; chose A 6B pencil for the darkest shadow under an apple; chose Charcoal for a big, dark stormy sky, fast; chose An eraser for the white shine on a shiny apple; chose An HB pencil for an even middle grey; made a pale guide line with the pencil; made a dark charcoal line with the charcoal; made the round edge of an apple with the pencil; made a long cast shadow with the charcoal
  • Show what you know
    1. What does tone mean? → how light or dark something is
    2. Which pencil draws the palest line? → 2H
    3. Why draw guide lines with a hard pencil? → because pale lines rub out easily later
    4. What is charcoal made from? → burnt wood
    5. Why is one side of the apple dark? → because it faces away from the light
    6. Shade is on the thing. A cast shadow… → falls on something else
    7. Which would you use for a big, dark sky, fast? → charcoal
    8. The brightest spot, facing the light, is the… → highlight

Make it for real

  • The colour wheel. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • Mixing across the wheel. Put out red, yellow and blue paint and a little white. Before each mix, ask: what do you think it will make? Mix it, look, and say what happened.
  • Which colour scheme?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Pick a colour scheme. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • Change the colour scheme. Look at something the child made. Ask: if you made it again, what one thing would you change? Then, if you can, change it together.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Paint a colour wheel. You need: Red, yellow and blue paint, a brush, water, and a paper plate. Paint red, yellow and blue round the plate, with gaps between them. In each gap, mix the two colours on either side and paint the new colour. Check: is each colour opposite its partner? Look for: Which colour sits opposite each primary?
  • Opposite mix. You need: Two opposite colours of paint, like red and green, a brush and paper. Paint a stripe of red and a stripe of green. Mix a little of each together and paint a stripe of the mix. Now paint red and green side by side, touching. Look for: Is the mix bright or dull? Do the colours look brighter side by side?
  • Scheme swap. You need: A simple drawing, copied twice, and crayons or paints. Colour the first copy in a warm scheme. Colour the second copy in a cool scheme. Put them side by side. Look for: How does the same drawing feel different?

Answer keys

  • The colour wheel find: Red is a primary colour. You cannot mix it from other colours.; Orange sits between red and yellow, because red and yellow make orange.; Blue sits opposite orange. Opposite colours are called complementary colours.; Green sits opposite red. Put red and green side by side and both look brighter.. Which colour sits opposite red on the wheel? → green

    • red + green → brown
    • blue + orange → brown
    • yellow + purple → brown
    then the child mixes any two they like: no key, the bowl shows what they made

    • a desert at midday (needs hot) → A warm scheme
    • a soft, gentle picture for a baby's room (needs gentle) → Soft tints
  • Change the colour scheme
    • Zain's fair poster: Nobody notices it from across the playground. → Use orange letters on a dark blue background
    • Lina's snowy day: It is painted in reds and oranges, and it feels hot, not cold. → Change to a cool scheme: blues, purples and white
    • Ravi's campfire: The fire is painted blue and green, and it looks cold. → Change to a warm scheme: red, orange and yellow
  • Colour wheel spotter
    1. Which colour sits opposite blue? → orange
    2. What do red and green make when you mix them? → brown
    3. Blue sea and green seaweed are a… → cool colour scheme
    4. Which scheme helps a poster stand out? → complementary colours

Where this art comes from

  • The clay brick is drawn in the manner of the pictorial bricks of Han dynasty China (206 BC to AD 220), most famously of the Eastern Han (AD 25 to 220) from tombs in Sichuan in the south-west. Their pictures of everyday life - horses and carriages, farming, markets and hunting - were pressed into the clay from carved moulds, so the picture stands up from the flat surface in low relief. The picture on the page is our own drawing in that manner, not a copy of a real brick.

Esther Mahlangu is one well-known Ndebele artist whose work is shown around the world. The shapes on the page are our own, not a copy of a real house or of any artist's design.

Make it for real

  • Does it meet the goal?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Praise and a next step. Look at a drawing the child did not make. Ask them to say one kind thing about it that is really there in the picture.
  • Change one thing. Look at something the child made. Ask: if you made it again, what one thing would you change? Then, if you can, change it together.
  • My journal, the whole year. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Make a portfolio. You need: A big sheet of card, folded in half, tape and pens. Fold the card to make a folder. Put in three pieces: a sketch, a finished piece and a note about a change. Write a label for each one. Look for: Which piece shows the most learning?
  • Feedback swap. You need: One of your pictures and a friend or grown-up. Ask them for one thing that works and one next step. Write both on a sticky note. Try the next step. Look for: Did the next step make it better?
  • Goal check. You need: Something you made this year. Remember what it was for: its goal. Ask: does it meet the goal yet? If not yet, change ONE thing. Look for: What did you change, and did it work?
