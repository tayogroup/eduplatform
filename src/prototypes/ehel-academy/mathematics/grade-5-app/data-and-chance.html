Data and Chance

Ask a question worth collecting data for, record answers in a frequency table, choose a chart that suits what you collected, summarise a set with its mode and its median, read a pattern back out of the numbers, and find out what a hundred trials tell you that ten cannot.

For the grown-up: what to have to hand

A bag or box with counters in two colours - ten of them, so the fractions in Step 52 are easy to say aloud. A coin for the trials step, because throwing a real one fifty times is slower than clicking and that slowness is the point. Squared paper for drawing the charts by hand.

Support and challenge
An easier way in

For the median, write the numbers on separate scraps of paper and physically sort them into order before looking for the middle. The mistake this prevents - reading the middle of the list as collected rather than the middle of the sorted list - is the one that causes most wrong answers here.

A harder challenge

Step 53 throws a fair coin. Predict, before running it, how far from half you expect to be at 10 throws and at 1000. Run each five times and write down the largest gap you see. Does the gap in THROWS grow or shrink as the trials increase, and does the gap as a PROPORTION do the same?

About

What this lesson is about

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Plan and conduct an investigation to answer a set of related statistical questions.
  • Record, organise and represent categorical, discrete and continuous data.
  • Understand that the mode and median are ways to describe and summarise data sets.
  • Interpret data, identifying patterns, within and between data sets, to answer statistical questions.
  • Use the language associated with likelihood to describe and compare likelihood and risk.
  • Recognise that some outcomes are equally likely to happen and some outcomes are more or less likely.
  • Conduct chance experiments or simulations, using small and large numbers of trials.
Lecture

Unit lecture

The whole lesson, in three parts.

Part 1 of 3

A question that varies

A statistical question is one where you expect the answers to differ. If everyone would answer the same, it is a fact to look up rather than data to collect - and a question nobody can check is an opinion, however much it varies.

Part 2 of 3

Two ways to say what a set is like

The mode is the value that turns up most often and the median is the one in the middle once the values are in order. Putting them in order is the step people skip, and with an even number of values the median can be a number that is not in the set at all.

Part 3 of 3

What the data can and cannot tell you

A chart can show that two things happened in the same week. It cannot show that one caused the other. Reading data well is partly about noticing the pattern and partly about not claiming more than the numbers support.

Step 47

A question worth asking

A statistical question is one where you expect the answers to VARY. If everyone would give the same answer, there is nothing to collect. Sort these into the ones worth a survey and the ones that are not.

Is this worth collecting data for?

Vary, and be answerable. "How many legs does a spider have?" has one answer, so it is a fact to look up rather than a question to survey. "What is the best song?" varies but has no answer anyone can check. A statistical question needs both: answers that differ, and a way of recording them.
Plan before you collect. Decide who you will ask, how many, and what the possible answers are. A survey with no list of answers gives you a pile of writing instead of data.
Step 48

Tally it, then count it

A frequency table turns a pile of answers into counts. Click a pupil to record their answer and watch the tally build; the frequency column is just the tally counted up.

Ask the next pupil

Frequency table

Group the strokes in fives. That is what makes a tally quicker to count than one long row — you count the complete groups in fives, then add the leftovers on the end. On paper the fifth stroke is usually drawn across the other four; here each group is shown as five marks with a gap after it.
Step 49

Which chart suits the data

The same numbers can be drawn more than one way, but not every way suits every kind of data. Switch between them and see what each one makes easy to read.

Show it as

What this chart is good for

Categories go in a bar chart, single values go in a dot plot. A dot plot puts one dot per item above its value, so it shows you where the data bunches up. A bar chart compares groups. Neither is wrong; they answer different questions.
Step 50

The mode and the median

Two ways to say what a set of numbers is "like". The mode is the value that appears most often. The median is the one in the middle once you put them in order — and putting them in order is the step people skip.

Choose a set

Working

With an even number of values there are two in the middle. The median is then halfway between them, which can be a number that is not in the set at all — and that is allowed.
Step 51

Reading the pattern back

Collecting data is only half the job. The other half is saying what it shows — and being careful not to say more than it shows.

Which statements does this data support?

A pattern is not a cause. The chart can tell you that more books were borrowed in the week of the reading club. It cannot tell you that the club is why — something else may have changed in the same week, and the data has no way of separating them.
Step 52

Likely, unlikely, certain

Chance has its own words, and they sit in order from impossible to certain. Look in the bag, then place the event on the line.

Drawing one counter without looking, it is…

Equally likely means the same number of ways, not "either it happens or it does not". A bag with 9 red and 1 blue still has two outcomes, and they are nowhere near equally likely. Count the counters, not the colours.
Step 53

Ten trials, or a hundred

A fair coin does not promise five heads in ten throws. Run the experiment and watch what happens to the proportion as the number of trials grows.

What happened

More trials does not make the counts even — it makes the PROPORTION settle. In a hundred throws you may still be several heads away from fifty, but as a fraction of the total that gap matters less and less. Ten throws can easily give 7 heads; a thousand almost never gives 700.
