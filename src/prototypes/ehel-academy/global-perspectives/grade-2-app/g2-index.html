Grade 2 Global Perspectives

Eight lessons, in the order they are meant to be done. Every one is about finding things out and talking about them: a question to build, a classmate to ask, a picture to read, a problem to solve, a garden to plant together. About 300 minutes in all, one lesson a week.

Lesson 1

Sharp Questions

Our playground is crowded at playtime. Ask questions that are focused on that problem, then find the exact part of a picture and the exact sentence of a text that answers each one.

Interview and Record

Interview six classmates twice: once to find information, once to find opinions. Record what you find as a pictogram, a bar chart and a table, and see that all three show the same thing.

Who Knows What

Different people know different things about our town. Work out who would know what, then give a short talk about the town where everything you say is about it.

Me and Others

What you do reaches other people. Predict what your action does to a classmate, watch it happen, then suggest something YOU could do about a problem of your own.

Sources and Reasons

More than one source can help with a topic. Suggest every source that would help find out about recycling, animals and the sea, say why, then say what you think with two reasons.

Our Team's Ideas

Plant the school vegetable patch and paint the assembly backdrop with a team. Do your own job, suggest an idea when the team is stuck, and then say which idea was yours and which was a friend's.

Talk It Through

Seven lessons of finding out, thinking, choosing, working together and talking. Look back over all of them: what did you learn, and which activity helped you learn it?

What Stage 2 Global Perspectives covers

ResearchAsking focused questions about the thing we want to find out; finding the part of a picture or a text that tells us; interviewing classmates to find information and opinions; and recording what we found in pictograms, tables and charts.
AnalysisKnowing that different people know different things about a topic; seeing that pictures, bars and numbers can all show information; thinking about what our actions do to OTHER people; and suggesting something we could do about a problem of our own.
EvaluationSuggesting the sources that would help with a topic, and saying why; and saying what we think about a topic with reasons.
ReflectionSaying which IDEA was ours and which was a friend's when a team finished a job; saying what we learned in an activity; and saying which part helped us learn.
CollaborationCarrying out our own job so the team's work gets done; and working positively with others towards something we all made.
CommunicationGiving a short talk about a topic where everything said is about it; and listening to others, then asking a simple, relevant question about what they said.

Do it together, for real

  • Build a focused question. Pick a topic (pets, the park, dinner). Take turns asking a question about it that starts with What, Where, Who, When, Why or How. Count how many different questions you can ask.
  • Find it in the picture. Look at one photo or picture book page together. Ask: what does this picture tell us? Find three things in it and say a fact about each.
  • Find it in the text. Read a short page of a non-fiction book together. Ask a question, and let the child find the ONE sentence that answers it and read it out.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Sharp questions at home. You need: A grown-up and a small problem at home. Pick a problem: the toys are everywhere, or breakfast takes too long. Ask three questions that are focused on that problem. Your grown-up says wide or focused for each one. Look for: Could each focused question be answered by looking, counting or asking?
  • Find it in the picture. You need: A busy picture in a book, and a grown-up. Your grown-up asks a question about the picture. Point to the exact part of the picture that answers it. Swap over and ask them one. Look for: Was the answer in one part of the picture, or the whole picture?
  • Find it in the text. You need: A leaflet, a menu or a notice, and a grown-up. Your grown-up asks a question the text can answer. Find the one sentence that answers it and read it out. Ask them a question back. Look for: Did you need to read all of it, or just find the right sentence?

Lesson 2: Interview and Record18 steps · about 40 minutes · 5 objectives

What it teaches (Cambridge Primary Global Perspectives 0838, Stage 2)

Do it together, for real

  • Interview: what do you play?. Ask everyone at home one question with a few answers (how do you get to work or school?). Draw one small picture per person in a row for each answer.
  • The same findings as a bar chart. Look at the picture rows you made. Ask: which row is the longest? How many chose this? Did anyone choose that?
  • Information or opinion?. Draw two big circles or two columns on paper and sort what you found out into them, one fact at a time, saying why each goes where it goes.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Interview your family. You need: Paper, a pencil, everyone at home. Ask everyone one information question: what do you eat for breakfast? Ask everyone one opinion question: should we have pancakes at the weekend? Record both. Put a tick per person for each answer. Look for: Which question found information? Which found opinions?
  • Three ways to show it. You need: Paper and crayons, and your breakfast findings. Draw your findings as a pictogram: one picture per person. Draw them again as bars. Write them as a table of numbers. Look for: Do all three show the same thing? Which was quickest to read?
  • Count the cars three ways. You need: A window onto a road, and five minutes. Make a mark for each car, bus and bike that passes. Draw the marks as bars. Write the numbers in a table. Look for: Which went past most? Could you tell from the bars before you counted?

Do it together, for real

  • Who would know?. Ask the child a question and listen for whether the answer is ABOUT the question. Swap: they ask, you answer with something off the point, and they catch you.
  • My talk about our town. Name a topic and take turns saying one thing you already know about it. Stop and laugh when somebody says something that is about a different topic.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Who knows what at home?. You need: Everyone at home. Think of three questions: how do we cook rice, what is for dinner, where is the nearest bus stop. For each one, say who at home would know best. Ask them and see if you were right. Look for: Did the same person know all three?
  • A talk about our street. You need: A grown-up to listen. Give a talk about your street: four sentences. Your grown-up puts a hand up if a sentence is not about the street. Try again until all four are about the street. Look for: Which sentence was hardest to keep on the topic?
  • Ask a person who knows. You need: A walk with a grown-up. On a walk, find a person doing a job: a shopkeeper, a driver, a gardener. With your grown-up, ask them one question about their job. Tell somebody at home what you found out. Look for: Could anybody else have answered that question as well?

    2. You want to know when the library opens. Who do you ask? → the librarian

Do it together, for real

  • What happens to them?. Talk about a small choice and what happens next: if you leave your coat, if you go to bed late, if you share your snack. What happens to YOU?
  • Something I could do. Name a small problem at home (toys everywhere, a thirsty plant) and offer three things you could do. Which one actually fixes it? Try it.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Who does it reach?. You need: Everyone at home, for one evening. When you do something, say out loud who it reaches: I put my shoes away, so nobody trips. Catch one thing that reached somebody in a bad way, and one in a good way. Tell everyone at dinner. Look for: Did anything you did today reach somebody you did not see?
  • My own problem, my own action. You need: A grown-up and a small problem of yours. Name a small problem you have: losing socks, forgetting your water bottle. Suggest three actions YOU could take. Not ones for your grown-up. Pick one and do it for a week. Look for: Did your action make a difference?
  • Help spotting. You need: A grown-up and a walk to the shops. Spot three things people do that help others: holding a door, moving aside, tidying. Spot one thing that hurts others. Say what each one did to the other people. Look for: Which helpful thing was the smallest? Did it still matter?
