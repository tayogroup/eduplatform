Step 14

The odd numbers, and what they add up to

The odd numbers are the simplest sequence of all: start at 1 and add 2 for ever. They are the numbers you cannot share into two equal piles, and on the strip below they are every other one. Two things are worth knowing about them, and neither needs counting. One is where any odd number sits in the list. The other is what happens when you add the first few of them up.

Step 2 showed this from the other side. There, each L you added to a square was the next odd number, which is why the squares grew 1, 4, 9, 16. Here is the same fact read as a list: add up the first few odd numbers and you always land exactly on a square. The other half of this step is the position. Counting along to the 50th odd number is slow, but 2 × 50 − 1 = 99 is quick. Going back the other way, (99 + 1) ÷ 2 = 50 tells you where 99 sits without counting a single one.
Step 15

The even numbers, and the rectangle they make

The even numbers are the other half of the strip: start at 2 and add 2 for ever. They are the numbers you CAN share into two equal piles, and that is the whole trick to them. Every row below is split down the middle into two equal halves, because every even number is a double. Step 14 added the odd numbers up and landed on a square. Do the same with the evens and you land somewhere just as tidy, but not the same shape.

Where does an even number sit? Halve it.

The evens against the odds

Odd numbers make a square, even numbers make a rectangle. The first four odd numbers add to 16, a 4 by 4 square. The first four even numbers add to 20, a 4 by 5 rectangle: one row taller. The gap between the two totals is never a surprise. Every even number is exactly one more than the odd number just before it. There are n of them, so the evens total is always n more. And because every even number is a double, adding them up is really adding the counting numbers twice over. 2 + 4 + 6 + 8 is two lots of 1 + 2 + 3 + 4. That is twice the triangle from Step 7.
Step 16

The order of the operations

3 + 4 × 5 has two possible answers. Work along the line and you get 35; do the multiplication first and you get 23. Only one of those is what the expression means. The way it is settled will be familiar from Step 21: it is a convention. Everybody agreed on it so that everybody reads the same line the same way, and nobody discovered it. The agreement is that × and ÷ are done before + and −.

An expression

23is the answer

What the agreement is worth

Operations of the same rank are done left to right, and that matters more than it looks. 10 − 2 − 3 is 5 because you take 2 away and then 3. Working from the right instead would give 10 − (2 − 3), which is 11. 12 ÷ 4 × 3 is 9 for the same reason, where right-to-left would give 1. So the rule is really two rules: multiplication and division first, and then equal ranks in reading order. Nothing here is a discovery. Every one of these expressions could have been given the other meaning if people had agreed differently. The convention exists because an expression that means two things is useless. Halfway rounding both ways would be useless in exactly the same way. And when the convention gives the wrong shape, brackets override it. If you really do want the 3 + 4 done first, you write (3 + 4) × 5, and the brackets say so out loud. That is the next thing after this one in the Cambridge sequence. It is the only way to change the order rather than merely complain about it.
Step 17

Factors and multiples

A factor of a number divides into it exactly, with nothing left over. A multiple of a number is what you get by counting it up: the times table. They point in opposite directions, and the difference between them is the thing to hold on to. The factors of a number are a short list that stops. Its multiples go on for ever. Factors also come in pairs, and where the two halves of a pair meet turns out to be the square root from Step 4.

Factors stop. Multiples do not. Every number has a first factor (1) and a last one (itself), so the list is finite and you can write all of it down. Nothing is the last multiple, so you write the first few and then three dots. The pairs are why you never have to test very far. Sorting the factors of 24 into pairs gives 1 × 24, 2 × 12, 3 × 8, 4 × 6. After 4 the pairs would just repeat backwards, because 4 × 6 and 6 × 4 are the same pair. The turning point is the square root, which is exactly the stopping rule Step 4 gave you. And a square number is the only kind with an odd number of factors. One of its pairs is a number paired with itself. 36 gives 1 × 36, 2 × 18, 3 × 12, 4 × 9 and then 6 × 6, which is nine factors, not ten. The next step is about the numbers at the other extreme — the ones with exactly two.
Step 18

What two numbers share

Put two numbers side by side and ask what they have in common. A common factor divides into both of them; a common multiple is in both times tables. Watch the names carefully, because they are not the same shape. We talk about the highest common factor but the lowest common multiple. Step 17 already explained why. Factor lists stop, so there is a highest one to find. Multiple lists never stop, so there is no highest — only a first.

Highest for factors, lowest for multiples — and the asymmetry is not a quirk of the words. Both factor lists stop, so the numbers in both stop too, and asking for the highest is a fair question with an answer. Neither multiple list stops, so their common ones go on for ever: 36, 72, 108, 144 and so on without end. There is nothing to call a highest common multiple, so the one worth naming is the first. Two shapes come up again and again. When one number divides the other, they have done the work for you. 8 and 16 share a highest factor of 8 and a lowest multiple of 16, the two numbers themselves. When they share nothing but 1, as 7 and 11 do, the lowest common multiple is simply the two multiplied together. And one thing is worth checking on every row of the table. The highest common factor times the lowest common multiple always comes to the two numbers multiplied. 6 × 36 = 216, and 12 × 18 = 216 as well.
Step 19

Divisibility rules

Step 17 asked what divides into a number, and answered it by trying every number in turn. For the small divisors you do not have to. Each one has a rule that reads only part of the number, sometimes a single digit. It tells you the answer without dividing at all. Pick a number and a divisor below, and the digits the rule actually looks at will light up. There is a rule for 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9 and 10. There is no useful one for 7, and that gap is worth knowing about.

Every rule at once, for

Three of the rules are really other rules wearing a hat. 6 is just 2 and 3 together, because a number divisible by both is divisible by their product. 9's rule is the same digit-sum test as 3's, asking for a multiple of 9 instead of 3. So anything that passes 9 passes 3 as well, and never the other way round. And 8's rule is the last three digits for the same reason 4's is the last two. 1,000 is a multiple of 8 and 100 is a multiple of 4. Everything above those columns divides cleanly and can be ignored. The digit-sum rules are the surprising ones, and there is a reason for them. 10 leaves 1 when you divide by 9, and so do 100 and 1,000 and every power after them. So the only thing a number leaves over when divided by 9 is what its digits add up to. That is why adding them works. It is also why the same trick is no use at all for 4 or 8. And then there is 7. No rule for it is quicker than dividing. That is why the next step settles 2, 3 and 5 at a glance, and then has to work out 47 ÷ 7 by hand. 7,315 is the case to look at: nothing visible about it suggests 7 goes into it, and 7 × 1,045 = 7,315.
Step 20

The prime numbers, the sequence with no rule

A prime number has exactly two factors: 1 and itself. Nothing else divides into it without a remainder. A number with more than two factors is called composite, which means put together. It can be built by multiplying smaller numbers, the way 21 is built from 3 and 7. There is no formula for them. You cannot write down the nth prime the way you can write 2n − 1 for the nth odd number. So instead of building the primes you find them, by crossing out everything that is not one. Start with the multiples of 2, which is the whole of Step 15 gone in one move.

The first twelve primes, and the gaps between them

Every whole number above 1 is one or the other. Exactly two factors and it is prime. More than two and it is composite, meaning it can be put together by multiplying smaller numbers. 21 is 3 × 7, 49 is 7 × 7, and every number this sieve crosses out is one. 1 is the single exception. That is why it sits dashed on the square rather than crossed out or shaded: with one factor it is neither. Every other sequence in this lesson has a rule. This one has none. The gaps go 1, 2, 2, 4, 2, 4, 2, 4, 6, 2, 6, 4. No pattern anybody has ever found, and that is not for want of looking. Three things are worth carrying away. 2 is the only even prime. Every other even number can be shared into two equal piles, which is exactly what Step 15 was about. A square number is never prime once you are past 1. n × n always has n as a factor: 49 has 7, 100 has 10. And the first number left standing after each crossing-out is the square of the next one to try: 9, then 25, then 49. That is why 7 is where you can stop on a hundred square. The next try would be 11, and 11 × 11 = 121 is already past 100.
