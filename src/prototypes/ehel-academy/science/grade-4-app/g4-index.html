What Stage 4 science covers

Thinking and Working ScientificallyModels and their limits, models of scale; the five enquiry types; variables in a fair test; keys; repeated measurements and reliable data; standard units; tables, bar charts and dot plots; patterns and conclusions.
BiologyThe bones and how muscles move them; skeletons, exoskeletons, vertebrates and invertebrates; medicines, vaccines and the importance of movement; energy from light and from food; habitats and surviving outside them; herbivores, carnivores, omnivores, predators and prey.
ChemistryThe particle model of solids and liquids, and particles always moving; materials, substances and particles; powders that pour; melting and freezing as physical changes; chemical reactions that make new substances.
PhysicsEnergy in everything, transferred but never lost; light in straight lines and ray diagrams; reflection and how we see; breaks and switches in circuits; brighter and dimmer lamps; conductors and insulators.
Earth and SpaceCrust, mantle and core; volcanoes and earthquakes at breaks in the crust; the spinning Earth, day and night and moving shadows; the planets, the Sun at the centre, asteroids and comets.
Science in ContextHow evidence changes what we know; science in the local area and the people who use it; using science to back up a point; the good and the harm science and technology can do nearby.

What it teaches (Cambridge Primary Science 0097, Stage 4)

  • 4Bs.01 Identify some of the important bones in the human body (limited to skull, jaw, rib cage, hip, spine, leg bones and arm bones).

  • 4Bs.02 Know that bones move because pairs of muscles that are attached to them contract and relax.

  • 4TWSa.01 Identify whether results support, or do not support, a prediction.
  • 4TWSa.03 Make a conclusion from results and relate it to the scientific question being investigated.
  • 4SIC.04 Identify people who use science, including professionally, in their area and describe how they use science.

Do the experiment for real

  • Muscles work in pairs. Your own arm. Hold the top of it with the other hand, bend the arm slowly and feel the biceps go short and hard; straighten it and feel the back of the arm tighten instead.
  • Feel the pair. You need: Your own arm and your other hand, or a grown-up's arm. Hold the top of your arm with your other hand. Or hold the top of a grown-up's arm while they bend it. Bend the arm slowly. Feel the front go short and hard: the biceps contracting. Straighten it slowly. Feel the back tighten instead: the triceps. Look for: One hard while the other is soft, then they swap. A pair.
  • Bone hunt on yourself. You need: A mirror, a grown-up. Find your skull, your jaw, your ribs, your spine, your hip and the bones in your arms and legs by pressing gently. Count how many bones you can feel in one finger, and then in your thumb. Find a joint where two bones meet. Look for: Three bones in each finger, and two in your thumb. How many joints did you find?
  • Look at a real bone. You need: A clean bone left over from a meal, or a picture of a bone in a book or on a screen, a grown-up. Look at the bone. It is hard on the outside. Look at the ends where it joined other bones. Draw it and label the hard outside and the joint ends. If you touched a real bone, wash your hands afterwards. Look for: An animal bone is built like yours: hard outside, and shaped at the ends to fit its neighbour.

Answer keys

  • Muscles work in pairs prediction: bend at the elbow; what happened: The biceps got short and fat and pulled the arm up; the triceps relaxed. Then they swapped
  • Bone check
    1. Which bone protects the brain? → the skull
    2. Which bone is a chain of small bones down your back? → the spine
    3. What does a muscle do when it contracts? → gets shorter and pulls
    4. When the biceps contracts, the triceps... → relaxes
    5. Which of these is not a job of the skeleton? → pumping blood
  • Show what you know
    1. Which is the biggest bone in your body? → the thigh bone in the leg
    2. Which bone moves when you chew? → the jaw
    3. Which bones join your legs to your spine? → the hip bones
    4. Can a muscle push a bone? → no, muscles can only pull
    5. Which muscle straightens the arm? → the triceps
    6. What would happen if your triceps stopped working? → you could bend your arm, but not straighten it
    7. Why is the rib cage shaped like a cage? → to protect the heart and lungs inside it
    8. What would you be without a skeleton? → a floppy heap that could not stand
    9. You put seven labels on the skeleton. What did you make? → a labelled diagram

Do the experiment for real

  • Minibeast key. You need: A grown-up, a garden, park or school ground, a magnifying glass if you have one, the key from this lesson written on paper. With a grown-up, look for four small creatures on plants, walls and paths, or under leaves. If you lift a stone, a grown-up helps: lift it slowly and put it back just as it was. Do not pick up or touch the creatures: count their legs by looking. No outdoor space? Use pictures of minibeasts in a book or on a screen instead. For each one, follow the key: legs? how many? wings? shell? Write the name the key gives you. Wash your hands when you come in. Look for: Did the key work for every creature? Which question was hardest to answer?
  • Backbone hunt. You need: Any animals you can see: pets, birds, a fish tank, a zoo, or pictures in a book. For each animal, decide: backbone or not? For the ones without, decide: a hard case over the whole body, or a soft body? A snail goes with the soft ones: it is soft, and carries a shell. Make three lists. Look for: Vertebrate, exoskeleton, soft invertebrate. Which list is longest?
  • Find a moult. You need: A grown-up, and a beach, a pond edge or a garden, or a book or website with pictures of moults. With a grown-up, look for an empty case shaped like a whole crab, or a split insect case on a plant stem. Keep away from deep water. If you cannot find one, look at a picture of one with a grown-up. Finding a real one takes luck. That is a moult: the old exoskeleton, left behind. Draw it, and draw what the animal looked like. Look for: The animal grew a bigger case and walked away from this one.

Answer keys

  • Vertebrate, or invertebrate? Vertebrate: dog, shark, frog, lizard, parrot; Invertebrate: snail, spider, jellyfish, bee, earthworm
  • Skeleton check
    1. An animal with a backbone is called... → a vertebrate
    2. Which of these is an invertebrate? → a snail
    3. What is an exoskeleton? → a hard skeleton covering the outside of the body
    4. Which group of animals has an exoskeleton? → insects
    5. What does a key do? → identifies a creature through yes-or-no questions
  • Show what you know
    1. Are you a vertebrate? → yes, you have a backbone
    2. A jellyfish is... → an invertebrate with no hard parts
    3. Where are a crab's muscles? → inside, pulling on the exoskeleton
    4. Why does a crab moult? → its exoskeleton cannot grow
    5. Six legs and wings. The key says... → butterfly
    6. Eight legs. The key says... → spider
    7. Which group has the most kinds of animal in the world? → invertebrates
    8. A snail has a hard shell. Why is it not a vertebrate? → it has no backbone inside its body
    9. Where did you find the facts about moulting? → in a fact card, a secondary source

Do the experiment for real

  • Medicine safety check. You need: A grown-up, the place your family keeps medicines. With a grown-up, look at where the medicines are kept. Are they high up or locked away? Read one label together. Find the dose and the date. Say the rules out loud. Look for: The right medicine, the right amount, a grown-up in charge, out of reach of little ones.
  • Heart rate before and after. You need: A clock or timer with seconds, a grown-up. Find your pulse: press two fingers gently on the inside of your wrist, below your thumb, or on the side of your neck. Each small beat you feel is one heartbeat. A grown-up can help you find it. Sit still and count the beats for thirty seconds. Now move quickly for two minutes in whatever way your body can: run on the spot, march, dance, or wave your arms fast. Count again. Write both numbers down. Look for: Faster after moving: your heart working harder, which is what makes it stronger. That number is evidence.
  • Vaccine record. You need: A grown-up, your health record or your pet's. Ask which vaccines you had as a baby. If you have a pet, find its vaccination card. List the diseases the vaccines protect against. Look for: Each one is a germ your body, or your pet's, already knows how to beat.

Answer keys

  • Medicines: safe, or not safe? Safe: a grown-up measures the dose on the label, medicines locked away high up, checking the date on the packet, finishing the whole course the doctor gave; Not safe: taking a friend's tablets, eating a pill because it looks like a sweet, taking double to get better twice as fast, medicine from a bottle with no label
  • Evidence, or opinion? Evidence: Doctors measured hearts: children who ran every day had stronger hearts, Counted over thousands of puppies, vaccinated ones almost never caught the disease, Scientists timed sleep: children who moved more fell asleep faster, Tests showed the medicine cured 9 out of 10 people with the illness; Opinion: I think PE is boring, My uncle says vaccines are a waste of time, Football is the best sport, Medicine always tastes horrible
  • Health check
    1. What is a medicine for? → treating an illness
    2. Who should give a child medicine? → a grown-up, measuring the amount on the label
    3. What does a vaccine do? → trains the body to fight a germ before it arrives
    4. Can a puppy be vaccinated? → yes, animals get infectious diseases too
    5. Why does moving every day help your heart? → the heart is a muscle, and it gets stronger with use
  • Show what you know
    1. You feel better halfway through a course of medicine. What should you do? → finish the course as the doctor said
    2. You find a pill on the floor that looks like a sweet. What should you do? → leave it and tell a grown-up
    3. What is an infectious disease? → one that spreads from one living thing to another
    4. When is a vaccine given? → when you are well, to protect you
    5. Can plants get infectious diseases? → yes, plants catch diseases too
    6. Which counts as moving every day? → walking, dancing and playing
    7. Which is evidence? → doctors measured that runners had stronger hearts
    8. Who uses medicine science in your area? → a pharmacist
    9. What would happen if nobody in a town was vaccinated against measles? → measles could spread from person to person much more easily
Lesson 4: Energy for Life14 steps · about 35 minutes · 6 objectives

Do the experiment for real

  • What did you eat?. You need: Paper and a pencil, your last meal. Write down everything you ate at your last meal. Next to each thing, write plant or animal. Decide: were you a herbivore, a carnivore or an omnivore at that meal? Look for: Every bit of energy in it started as sunlight.
  • Predator watch. You need: A window or a garden, ten minutes. Watch for a bird, a cat, a spider or a beetle. For each one, ask: what does it hunt? What hunts it? Draw the chain with arrows. Look for: A spider is a predator of flies and prey to a bird. Both at once.
  • A food chain model. You need: Three cards, string, a pencil. Draw a plant, a herbivore that eats it, and a carnivore that eats that. Tie them in a line with string, plant first. Label each: producer, herbivore, carnivore, predator, prey. Look for: Your model shows the relationship. Real animals eat many things; the model leaves that out.

Answer keys

  • Herbivore, carnivore or omnivore? Herbivore: rabbit, elephant, horse, snail; Carnivore: lion, shark, owl; Omnivore: bear, human, pig
  • Energy check
    1. Where does a plant get its energy? → from light
    2. Where does a fox get its energy? → from eating other animals
    3. An animal that eats only plants is... → a herbivore
    4. An animal that eats both plants and animals is... → an omnivore
    5. An animal that hunts others is called... → a predator
    6. Why do you need to eat? → for the energy to grow, move and stay healthy
  • Show what you know
    1. What does every living thing need to grow and move? → energy
    2. Which of these is a producer? → grass
    3. An elephant eats only plants. It is a... → herbivore
    4. A bear eats berries and fish. It is a... → omnivore
    5. In the chain seeds → mouse → owl, the mouse is the... → prey
    6. Can an animal be both predator and prey? → yes, a frog eats flies and herons eat frogs
    7. What did the food chain you built show? → who eats whom in grass, rabbit and fox
    8. Where did the fox's energy come from, in the very beginning? → the Sun, through the grass and the rabbit
    9. In the chain grass → rabbit → fox, what would happen to the foxes if all the grass died? → they would go hungry, because the rabbits would have nothing to eat

Do the experiment for real

  • Damp or dry?. You need: A grown-up, two patches of ground side by side in the same shade, two flat pieces of wood the same size, a cup of water, a week. With a grown-up, put a piece of wood on each patch. No ground outside? Do it in a park with a grown-up, or choose another project. Every day, pour a cup of water on one patch to keep it damp. Leave the other patch dry. After a week, a grown-up lifts both pieces at the same time. Count the woodlice under each, then put the wood back gently. Write the two numbers down, and wash your hands. Look for: More under the damp one? Same shade, same size, same time: only the dampness was different. That is a fair comparison.
  • Suited features. You need: A pet, or a picture of an animal, paper. Draw the animal. Label three features: feet, covering, mouth, tail. Next to each, write how it suits where the animal lives. Look for: A cat's claws, whiskers and night eyes suit a hunter.
  • Good and bad near you. You need: A walk round your street with a grown-up, paper. With a grown-up, find three things people have built: a road, lights, a pond, a bin. For each, write one good effect on living things and one bad effect. Decide which one has done the most good. Look for: Science helps you see both sides before you decide.

Answer keys

  • Which habitat suits it? Desert: camel, desert lizard; Arctic: polar bear, reindeer, Arctic fox; Rainforest: monkey, parrot, tree frog; Ocean: whale, octopus
  • Back it up with science
    1. "We should keep the old hedge by the school." Which reason uses science? → birds nest in it: it is their habitat
    2. "Move the fish tank away from the sunny window." Which reason uses science? → the water would get too warm for the fish
    3. "Leave the woodlice under the log." Which reason uses science? → woodlice need damp, dark places to survive
    4. "A cactus is suited to the desert." Which evidence supports that? → it stores water in its thick stem and has spines
  • Habitat check
    1. Which feature suits a camel to the desert? → a hump that stores fat
    2. Which feature suits a polar bear to the Arctic? → thick fur over a layer of fat
    3. Can a pet goldfish survive in a bowl? → yes, if a person feeds it and keeps the water clean
    4. Why can a zoo keep a seal far from the sea? → it copies the habitat with a pool
    5. Why would a fish not survive in a desert? → its gills need water to breathe
  • Show what you know
    1. What does suited to a habitat mean? → its features fit the place, so it survives there
    2. Which animal is suited to the rainforest? → a monkey
    3. Camels from Asia now live wild in Australia. What made that possible? → its hot dry centre is like their desert home
    4. A rainforest plant on a windowsill survives if... → it is watered and kept warm
    5. Which is a bad effect of streetlights on living things? → moths and bats get confused by them
    6. Which is a good effect of a park pond? → a new habitat for frogs and dragonflies
    7. Which question about a habitat could you investigate? → are more woodlice found in damp places than dry?
    8. A zoo keeps a penguin far from its home. What must the zoo give it? → food, water, the right temperature and shelter
    9. Why should you never let a pet goldfish go in a pond or river? → it can survive there and eat food the wild animals need

Do the experiment for real

  • Drop them three times each. A paper spinner (a strip of paper cut and folded into two wings and a stem, with a paperclip at the bottom) and a stopwatch. Drop it from the same height three times and time each drop.
  • Make and drop a spinner. You need: A strip of paper about 20 centimetres long and 5 centimetres wide, scissors, a paperclip, a stopwatch or timer, a tape measure, a grown-up. Ask a grown-up to help with the scissors. Cut down the middle of the strip from the top, about 8 centimetres, to make two wings. Just below the wings, make a small cut in from each side, and fold the two sides in over each other to make a thin stem. Fold up the bottom of the stem and clip the paperclip on it. Fold one wing forwards and the other backwards. Stand on the floor, not on a chair, and drop it from exactly one metre, three times, timing each drop. Write the three times down. Look for: Are they close? Which is the odd one, if any? What is your reliable answer?
  • Change the wings. You need: Two spinners, one with wings twice as long, the same paper and clip. Drop each spinner three times from the same height. Write down all six times. Compare the two clusters. Look for: That is the fair test. Which spinner fell more slowly? What was the one thing you changed?
  • Dot plot on the fridge. You need: Paper, a pencil, your six times. Draw a line and mark times along it every half second, starting at 0: 0, 0.5, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 3.5, 4.0 seconds. Put a dot above the line for each drop, one colour per spinner. Write your conclusion under it. Look for: Two clusters, and the answer to the question, on one sheet.

Answer keys

  • Which type of enquiry? Fair test: Does a spinner with bigger wings fall more slowly?, Does more water make a plant grow taller?; Observing over time: How does frogspawn change each week?, Does the Moon look the same every night this month?; Classifying: Which of these leaves are from an oak?, Which of these things are magnetic?; Pattern seeking: Do taller people jump further?; Research: How far away is Mars?
  • Change, measure, or keep the same? Change: the size of the wings; Measure: how long it takes to fall, the number of seconds on the stopwatch; Keep the same: the height we drop it from, the paperclip on the bottom, the kind of paper, who drops it, whether the window is open
  • Choose the equipment
    1. To time how long the spinner falls, use... → a stopwatch, in seconds
    2. To make sure every drop is from the same height, use... → a tape measure, in centimetres
    3. Why use seconds and centimetres rather than counting or hand spans? → they are the same for everyone, so results can be compared
    4. To make the two spinners' wings exactly the sizes you planned, use... → a ruler and scissors
  • Drop them three times each prediction: The one with bigger wings; what happened: Small wings: 2.1, 2.3 and 2.0 seconds. Bigger wings: 2.8, 3.0 and 2.7 seconds
  • Investigation check
    1. Does a bigger spinner fall slower? What type of enquiry? → a fair test
    2. In our fair test, what did we change? → the size of the wings
    3. What did we keep the same? → the height, the paper, the paperclip and who dropped it
    4. Why drop each spinner three times? → repeats show which numbers to trust
    5. Why time it in seconds? → a standard unit everyone can compare
    6. Which chart shows repeated measurements of the same thing? → a dot plot
  • Show what you know
    1. Which is observing over time? → drawing the Moon every night for a month
    2. What is a variable? → anything in the test that could change
    3. You change the wing size and the drop height. Is it a fair test? → no; you cannot tell which change made the difference
    4. Three drops gave 2.1, 2.3 and 2.0. Was one of them a mistake? → no; small differences like these are normal
    5. Three drops gave 2.1, 2.2 and 9.0. Which do you doubt? → the 9.0, the odd one out
    6. What did the dot plot show? → the three drops clustering close to 2.1 seconds
    7. What is the conclusion of our test? → bigger wings fall more slowly, and the repeats show it reliably
    8. Which unit did you measure the drop height in? → centimetres
    9. Why must both spinners be dropped from the same height? → so the wings are the only thing that changes
