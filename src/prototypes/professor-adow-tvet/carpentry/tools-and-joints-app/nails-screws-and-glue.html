Nails, Screws and Glue

0

What this lesson is about

Tell nails apart by head and section, choose a screw by its head, drive a fastener deep enough to hold, pre-drill where the timber would split, glue a joint without flooding it, and inspect the finished work against the drawing.

By the end of this lesson you can:

  1. Select a nail for a job from its head and its section.
  2. Select a screw by head type: countersunk, round head, coach screw.
  3. Apply the rule that two thirds of a fastener should enter the lower member.
  4. Pre-drill in hardwood and near the end of a piece.
  5. Apply glue as a thin even film rather than a flood or a few spots.
  6. Clean up and inspect the finished work against the drawing before passing it on.
Where this leadsThis is foundation work. It is not a unit of the Kenyan Carpentry & Joinery Level 4 qualification — all eight of those units assume you already have tools and joints. What you learn here is what they are built on: marking out, sawing to a line and fitting a joint feed directly into Doors and Windows (0732 351 05D), Timber Furniture Items (0732 351 06D) and Cabinets (0732 351 08D).

The nails

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereChoosing a nail by length alone and taking whatever is in the tin. Two nails of the same length behave completely differently. A wire nail in the end of a piece of hardwood splits it; an oval brad in the same place does not. The section is not a detail.

The screws

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereDriving a countersunk screw into an un-countersunk hole and leaning on it. The tapered head acts as a wedge. It will either stand proud, or it will split the timber as it forces its way down. Countersink the hole, and the head pulls the two members together instead of pushing them apart.

How deep is deep enough?

Assessed here · and at the bench

A nail through an upper member into a lower one. Slide it in.

What goes wrong hereJudging by whether the head is down rather than by how much is in the lower member. A short fastener can look perfectly driven — head flush, nothing showing — and be holding almost nothing, because nearly all of its length is in the piece it is meant to be holding DOWN rather than in the one it is holding down TO.

How much glue?

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereUsing plenty on the grounds that more glue means a stronger joint. It does not. A thin continuous film is stronger than a thick one, and the excess squeezes out under the cramps, runs down the work and seals the grain where it dries — so the finish will not take, and the mark shows for the life of the piece.
