Ehel Academy · Global Perspectives

Grade 3 Global Perspectives

Eight lessons, in the order they are meant to be done. Every one is about finding things out and talking about them: a question to build, a classmate to ask, a picture to read, a problem to solve, a garden to plant together. About 295 minutes in all, one lesson a week.

Lesson 1

Our Own Questions

Water comes out of the tap, but where from, and where does it go? Construct your own questions to understand it, then find the answers inside a text and a picture.

17 steps · about 40 min · stickersStart Lesson 2

Observe and Measure

Three ways to investigate: observe and count what goes past the school gate, ask the class a questionnaire, and measure the bean plants with a ruler. Then record what you found by how you found it.

18 steps · about 35 min · stickersStart Lesson 3

What the Data Says

A chart is not the end of an investigation. Survey the class about snacks, draw conclusions the data proves, tell a conclusion from a guess, and sort snacks on a Venn diagram.

17 steps · about 35 min · stickersStart Lesson 4

People Think Differently

Should dogs run free in our park? The park keeper, a dog owner, a parent and a runner all think differently. Work out who thinks what, find the author's viewpoint in a poster and a letter, and give your own opinion about somebody else's, with reasons.

17 steps · about 40 min · stickersStart Lesson 5

Causes and Actions

Why did you do it, and what did it do to somebody else? Find the cause behind an action and its consequence for others, then suggest actions of your own for problems that are other people's.

17 steps · about 40 min · stickersStart Lesson 6

Team Roles

A team gives out its jobs so each goes to the person who can do it. Allocate the jobs for the fair sign and the recycling garden, bring ideas when the team is stuck, then look honestly at what you did well and what took more than one go.

16 steps · about 35 min · stickersStart Lesson 7

Present It

A talk needs a start, a middle and an end. Build two talks with that structure, listen to three classmates present and respond with relevant ideas and questions, and construct questions to understand a talk better.

17 steps · about 40 min · stickersStart Lesson 8

Look Back

Seven lessons of investigating, thinking, weighing up, working together and presenting. Look back over all of them: what did you learn, how did your ideas change, and which kinds of activity helped you learn?

15 steps · about 30 min · stickersStart

What Stage 3 Global Perspectives covers

ResearchConstructing our own questions to understand a topic; finding the information and the answers inside a source; investigating by interviewing, observing and measuring; and selecting, organising and recording what we found in charts and diagrams.
AnalysisKnowing that people think and believe different things about a topic; drawing conclusions from charts and numbers; talking about the causes of what we do and its consequences for others; and suggesting actions that could make a difference to an issue affecting other people.
EvaluationDiscussing a source and seeing that its author has a viewpoint; and giving an opinion about somebody else's viewpoint, with reasons.
ReflectionSaying what we did well in a team and what we could do better; saying how working together made the outcome better; saying what we learned and how our ideas changed; and saying which kinds of activity help us learn.
CollaborationGiving out the team's jobs so each goes to the right person; and bringing ideas to the team while working positively with everyone in it.
CommunicationPresenting information about a topic clearly, with a start, a middle and an end; and listening to others, then responding with relevant ideas and questions.

Do it together, for real

  • Make your own questions about water. Pick a topic (pets, the park, dinner). Take turns asking a question about it that starts with What, Where, Who, When, Why or How. Count how many different questions you can ask.
  • Find the answers in the text. Read a short page of a non-fiction book together. Ask a question, and let the child find the ONE sentence that answers it and read it out.
  • Find it in the kitchen. Look at one photo or picture book page together. Ask: what does this picture tell us? Find three things in it and say a fact about each.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Questions I cannot answer yet. You need: A grown-up and a topic at home: the fridge, the water tank, the bins. Make three questions about it that you cannot answer yet. Say which is a where-from, a what-happens or a how-much question. Ask your grown-up, or find out together. Look for: Did the answers help you understand how it works?
  • Find the answer in the text. You need: A leaflet, a label or a page, and a grown-up. Your grown-up asks a question the text can answer. Find the one sentence that answers it and read it out. Ask them one that the text CANNOT answer, and say where you would look instead. Look for: Which sentence held the answer? Was it where you expected?
  • Water at home. You need: A grown-up, a measuring jug and the kitchen. Find where clean water comes into your home. Find where used water goes out. Measure how much water fills the kettle. Look for: How many litres? Was it more or less than you guessed?

Do it together, for real

  • Observe at the gate. Stand at a window or a gate for five minutes and count one kind of thing - red cars, people with dogs, buses. Make a mark for each. Then count a second kind and compare.
  • What the gate count shows. Look at the picture rows you made. Ask: which row is the longest? How many chose this? Did anyone choose that?
  • Ask: how long is your journey?. Ask everyone at home one question with a few answers (how do you get to work or school?). Draw one small picture per person in a row for each answer.
  • How did we find it out?. Draw two big circles or two columns on paper and sort what you found out into them, one fact at a time, saying why each goes where it goes.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Observe at a window. You need: A window onto a road or a path, five minutes, paper and pencil. Count one kind of thing: red cars, or people with bags, or birds. Make a mark for each one. Then count a second kind and draw both as bars. Look for: Which passed more? By how many?
  • A questionnaire at home. You need: Everyone at home. Ask everyone the same question with three choices: how long does your journey to work or school take? Record every answer in a table. Say which choice most people picked. Look for: Could you have found that out by observing? Why not?
  • Measure and compare. You need: A ruler and three things: a spoon, a book, a shoe. Measure each one in centimetres. Put them in order, shortest to longest. Say how much longer the longest is than the shortest. Look for: Was your guess before measuring right?

    2. How many children have a journey over 15 minutes? → 2
    3. How many children answered altogether? → 6
  • Measure the bean plants
    1. Whose plant is the TALLEST? → Nora's plant
    2. How tall is Amal's plant, in centimetres? → 12
    3. How many centimetres taller is Nora's plant than Sami's? → 7
    4. Whose plant is the SHORTEST? → Sami's plant
  • How did we find it out? Observed: Five walkers passed the gate, Four cars passed the gate; Asked: Sami's journey takes twenty minutes, Nora lives across the road; Measured: Nora's plant is 15 cm tall, Sami's plant is 8 cm tall
  • Observing, asking, measuring
    1. How many buses pass the gate? Best way to find out? → observe and count at the gate
    2. How long is Sami's journey? Best way? → ask him on a questionnaire
    3. How tall is a bean plant? Best way? → measure with a ruler

  • Show what you know
    1. What does OBSERVE mean? → look carefully and count what is really there
    2. How many more walkers than cars passed the gate? → 1

Do it together, for real

  • Survey: your favourite break-time snack. Ask everyone at home one question with a few answers (how do you get to work or school?). Draw one small picture per person in a row for each answer.
  • Draw conclusions from the bars. Look at the picture rows you made. Ask: which row is the longest? How many chose this? Did anyone choose that?
  • Liked, healthy, or both?. Draw two big circles or two columns on paper and sort what you found out into them, one fact at a time, saying why each goes where it goes.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?

Say three conclusions the bars prove: the most, how many altogether, how many more. Say one sentence the bars do NOT prove, and why. Look for: Could somebody argue with your conclusions? Not if the data proves them.
  • A Venn diagram of toys. You need: Paper, a pencil, and a box of toys. Draw two overlapping circles: toys with wheels, toys that are soft. Put each toy in the right part. A soft toy car goes in the middle. Count how many are in each part. Look for: Was the middle empty, or full?
  • Read a real table. You need: A bus timetable, a food label or a football table, and a grown-up. Find two numbers you can compare. Say which is bigger, and by how much. Say one conclusion the table proves. Look for: Did you need the whole table, or just two numbers?

Lesson 4: People Think Differently17 steps · about 40 minutes · 5 objectives

What it teaches (Cambridge Primary Global Perspectives 0838, Stage 3)

  • 3Ap.01 Recognise that people think or believe different things about a topic
  • 3Es.01 Discuss a source, recognising that the author has a clear viewpoint on the topic
  • 3Ea.01 Express an opinion about another person’s viewpoint, giving reasons for opinion
  • 3Fv.01 Talk about what has been learned during an activity and consider how personal ideas have changed
  • 3Fl.01 Identify which types of activities support learning

Do it together, for real

  • Who thinks what?. Ask the child a question and listen for whether the answer is ABOUT the question. Swap: they ask, you answer with something off the point, and they catch you.
  • The park keeper's poster. Read a short page of a non-fiction book together. Ask a question, and let the child find the ONE sentence that answers it and read it out.
  • What do YOU think of their views?. Ask: what do you think about ___? Listen for an opinion AND a reason, and make sure the reason is about the topic.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Who thinks what at home?. You need: Everyone at home and a question with no right answer. Ask: should we have a pet? Or: should bedtime be later? Listen to everyone's view and their reason. Say who thinks what, without saying who is right. Look for: Did anybody change their view after hearing the others?
  • Find the author's viewpoint. You need: A leaflet, an advert or a letter, and a grown-up. Work out who wrote it. Find the sentence that shows what they want you to think or do. Say their viewpoint in one sentence. Look for: Would a different author have written it differently?
  • My opinion, two reasons. You need: A grown-up. Your grown-up says a view: children should not have sweets on school days. Say whether you agree, partly agree or disagree. Give two reasons that are about sweets and school days. Look for: Were both your reasons about the topic?

Answer keys

  • Before we start a starting check, not marked: Which one is a fact? → The park has a pond.; A farmer and a football team see the same rainy day. Can they think different things about it? → Yes: people can think different things
  • Who thinks what?
    1. Who thinks dogs should be on leashes because they frighten the ducks? → Mr Bello, the park keeper
    2. Who thinks dogs need to run free to be healthy? → Mrs Adams, the dog owner
    3. Who thinks a fenced dog area would suit everyone? → Mr Khan, the dad
    4. Who thinks dogs off the leash trip runners up on the path? → Leila, the runner
  • A fact, or what somebody thinks? A fact: The park has a pond with ducks on it, Some dogs dig in the flower beds, Runners use the path round the pond; What somebody thinks: Dogs should always be on leashes in the park, Dogs need to run free to be healthy, A fenced dog area would suit everyone
  • The park keeper's poster
    1. Which sentence shows what the author WANTS people to do? → So please keep your dog on a leash at all times.
    2. Which sentence gives the author's reason about the DUCKS? → The ducks on our pond are frightened by loose dogs, and three nests were lost this year.
    3. Which sentence tells you WHO the author is? → Thank you. Mr Bello, Park Keeper.
    4. What is the author's viewpoint? → Dogs should be kept on leashes in the park
  • The dog owner's letter
    1. Which sentence says what the author thinks the park SHOULD do? → I think the park should let dogs run free on the big field, away from the pond.
