## Overview

Grade 1 English is the first full year of English at Ehel Academy, written for
children who are five or six and, for many, meeting written English for the
first time. It follows **Cambridge Primary English 0058, Stage 1**.

The year begins before Unit 1. A short readiness check finds each child's
starting point, and Unit 0, *Alphabet & Sounds*, teaches the letters and their
sounds over six weeks so that no child is asked to read words before they know
the letters those words are made of. Ten units then follow across three terms,
each built the same way: a handful of new words met through pictures and play, a
story to listen to and join in with, six short activities, some talking, some
tracing and writing, and a small piece of work to be proud of at the end.

By the end of the year a child will have met **366 English words**, listened to
and read **36 short texts**, and worked through **66 learning outcomes**. The
year closes with a capstone — a picture-and-word portfolio, a six-page mini-book
and a one-minute presentation — and a final course quiz.

This is a course a child does *with* a grown-up. Recordings, labels, drawings
and reflections all assume someone is sitting alongside. That is by design at
this age, and the policies below say plainly where an adult is needed.

## Course policies

### Prerequisites

No prior English is required, and none is assumed.

Before Unit 1 every child takes the **Grade 1 English Readiness Check**: 12
questions, about 10 minutes, covering letters and sounds, first English words
and classroom English. Attempts are unlimited and it is **never a fail** — it
places, it does not judge. It reports one of four results, and each carries a
next step rather than a verdict:

- **Ready for Grade 1** — start at Unit 1.
- **Ready, with review** — start at Unit 1, with Unit 0 alongside for the
  letters still settling.
- **Not yet ready** — begin at Unit 0, *Alphabet & Sounds*, which is part of
  this course and needs no separate enrolment.
- **A section needs attention** — the report names the section and links either
  to Unit 0 or to the Intensive English foundation course.

A child who has not met the alphabet has not fallen behind. Unit 0 exists for
exactly that child and runs for six weeks.

### Materials and equipment

For the child, per unit:

- Something to draw and trace with — pencils and colours.
- A folder or scrapbook to keep finished work in. This becomes the capstone
  portfolio in Unit 10, so keep the work from Unit 1 onward.
- Scissors, glue and scrap paper for the making units (Unit 4, *Making Things*,
  and the Unit 10 mini-book).
- Occasional household objects the units ask for by name — a piece of fruit, a
  toy, an item of clothing. Nothing needs buying.

For the household:

- A device that plays audio, with working sound. Much of Grade 1 is listening.
- A way to record short audio or video. Assignments ask the child to say two or
  three sentences aloud, and the recording is the submission.
- An internet connection for live sessions.

Headphones help a child hear the sounds of the letters clearly, and help more
than anything else on this list.

### Attendance

The course runs **90 live sessions** across the year, of 30 and 50 minutes.

- **Unit 0** carries 30 sessions across its six weeks — the heaviest teaching in
  the year, because letters and sounds are taught, not discovered.
- **Units 1–10** carry 6 sessions each, across two weeks per unit.

Attendance is expected at every session. A child of this age loses the thread
quickly, and the sessions are where new words are first heard said correctly.

If a child cannot attend, tell the teacher in advance where possible. Sessions
are recorded, and a missed session should be watched before the next one rather
than left to accumulate. If a child misses more than two sessions in a unit, the
teacher will contact the family to agree how to catch up — that is a
conversation, not a sanction.

### Homework

Homework in Grade 1 is short, practical and always finishable in one sitting.

Each unit carries **six activities** and **one assignment**. The assignment is
the piece of work that comes home. Across the year that is **11 assignments** —
one per unit, with the Unit 10 capstone counting double.

An assignment typically asks the child to:

1. Draw something, or find a real object.
2. Label it with two traced words.
3. Record themselves saying two or three sentences.
4. Answer one question a grown-up writes down for them.

Step 4 is the grown-up's part, and it is genuinely part of the task — a
five-year-old is not expected to write their own reflection. Expect around
**20–30 minutes per assignment**, spread over the two weeks of the unit, not
done in one evening.

Late work is accepted. At this age a missed deadline is a household's week, not
a child's character.

### Assessment and grading

Nothing in Grade 1 is graded by comparison with other children.

**Assignments — 288 marks across the year.** Ten unit assignments at 24 marks
each, and the Unit 10 capstone at 48. Each is marked against a four-level
rubric, where level 1 is *needs a full spoken model* and level 4 is *uses a
clear complete sentence*. A child moving from level 2 to level 3 across the year
is exactly what this course is for.

**Unit quizzes — 110 questions across the year**, roughly ten per unit. Short,
immediate, and adult-supported.

**Final course quiz.** After the Unit 10 capstone: 30 questions, 30 marks, about
25 minutes, in three sections — words and listening, patterns and reading, and
communication and care. The pass mark is **80%**, attempts are unlimited with
adult-supported review, and feedback is immediate with a final mastery report.

**Self-assessment.** Each unit ends with six *I can…* statements the child rates
themselves. These carry no marks and are never used to rank a child. They are
there so a five-year-old learns to notice what they have learnt.

### Behaviour and participation

Grade 1 English is a talking course, and the expectations are matched to age:

- **Join in.** Repeat the words, sing along with the story, put a hand up. A
  wrong answer said out loud is worth more here than a right answer kept quiet.
- **Take turns and listen.** Live sessions are small and every child speaks.
- **Try in English first.** Reaching for a first-language word when stuck is
  fine and expected; the teacher will supply the English one.
- **Cameras on** in live sessions where the household can manage it, so the
  teacher can see whether a child is following. Tell the teacher if that is
  difficult and an arrangement will be made.
- **A grown-up nearby.** Not doing the work — nearby, for the recordings and the
  parts that need a hand.

Mistakes in pronunciation are how this year works. No child is corrected in a
way that makes them reluctant to speak again.

### Support and communication

**If a child is struggling**, say so early. Unit 0 stays open all year and can
be revisited at any point; the readiness check can be retaken as often as
useful; and the Intensive English foundation course is available for a child who
needs a slower on-ramp to letters and sounds.

**Extra help available:**

- The teacher, at the contact above.
- Wehel, the in-course AI tutor, available while the child is working.
- Unit audio — every word, story and overview is recorded, so a child can hear
  a word said correctly as many times as they need.

**How the school will contact you:** after a missed unit assignment, after two
missed live sessions in a unit, and at the end of each term with a short summary
of what the child can now do.

**If you have a concern**, contact the teacher first. If it is unresolved after
{{n}} school days, contact {{school administrator role/name}}.

**For English as an additional language, or any additional need** — speech
delay, hearing, attention, anything at all — tell the teacher at the start of
the year rather than waiting for difficulty. Grade 1 is easy to adjust and hard
to repair.

## Before submitting for approval

1. Fill every `{{...}}` — an unfilled placeholder is the most common reason a
   syllabus is returned.
2. Confirm term dates on the school's academic terms, not here. The syllabus
   pulls them from `local_prequran_acad_term` at render time.
3. Save as **draft**, then submit for approval. Only a school administrator can
   approve, never the author.
4. Set visibility deliberately. `enrolled` (students and parents) is the
   default; `public` puts it in front of prospective families; `staff` hides it
   from the families it was written for.
5. Editing an approved syllabus sends it back to draft and it must be approved
   again. That is intended — an approved syllabus is a durable artifact.
