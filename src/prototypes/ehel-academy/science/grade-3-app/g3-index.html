What Stage 3 science covers

Thinking and Working ScientificallyThe five types of enquiry; asking questions that can be tested; predicting, staying safe, choosing equipment, measuring in standard units, looking things up, recording in tables and diagrams; patterns, conclusions and bar charts; diagrams and physical models.
BiologyWhat the parts of a flowering plant do and what a plant needs; the groups of animals and what marks each one out; the brain, heart, lungs, stomach and intestine; living, once alive and never alive; how young animals grow up; food chains.
ChemistrySolids, liquids and gases and how they differ; mixtures, and separating them with a sieve, a magnet or a filter; dissolving.
PhysicsMeasuring forces with a forcemeter; gravity; friction on rough and smooth surfaces; light through transparent, translucent and opaque materials; shadows and their size; magnets, their poles, attracting and repelling.
Earth and SpaceEarth as the source of every material we use; fossils; the Moon's changing shape, and how the Earth and Moon move; Earth, Sun and Moon as spheres.
Science in ContextHow thinking has changed, how the things we use work, who uses science at work, and how what we do affects the world.

Do the experiment for real

  • Living, once alive, never alive hunt. You need: Paper and a pencil, your kitchen. Find ten things in the kitchen. For each one, ask: is it alive now? Did it come from something alive? Or neither? Draw three lists. Look for: Wooden spoons, paper, cotton cloths and leather were all once alive.
  • Watch a bean over time. You need: A dried bean, a clear plastic cup, wet kitchen paper, a notebook. Put the bean between the wet paper and the side of the cup, so you can see it. Every day, draw what you see and write the date. Keep going for two weeks. Look for: That is observing over time. Which day did the root appear? Which day the shoot?
  • A fair test with paper towels. You need: Two different paper towels, a spoon, water, a tray. Cut one piece of each towel. Make the two pieces the same size. Put the two pieces on the tray. Put exactly one spoonful of water on each. Lift each towel and see how much water is left on the tray. Look for: Change only the towel. Keep the size and the water the same. That is what makes it fair.

Answer keys

  • Living, once alive, or never alive? Living: a cat, an oak tree, a mushroom; Once alive: a wooden chair, a leather shoe, a paper book, a woollen jumper; Never alive: a stone, a plastic cup, a drinking glass
  • Which type of enquiry? Fair test: Does a plant grow taller with more water?, Which surface is the slipperiest?; Observing over time: How does a tadpole change each week?, Does the Moon look the same every night?; Classifying: Which of these animals are insects?, Sort these rocks by how hard they are; Pattern seeking: Do taller children have bigger feet?; Research: How far away is the Moon?
  • Alive, or not?
    1. Which of these do plants and animals both do? → grow
    2. A wooden spoon is... → once alive
    3. A metal key is... → never alive
    4. You want to know which paper towel soaks up the most water. Which enquiry? → a fair test
    5. Making more of your own kind is called... → reproduction
  • Show what you know
    1. How many life processes did you learn? → four: nutrition, growth, movement, reproduction
    2. A leather shoe is... → once alive
    3. A mushroom is... → living
    4. Which of these was never alive? → a glass bottle
    5. How does a plant move? → it turns its leaves and flowers towards the light
    6. Drawing the Moon every night for a month is... → observing over time
    7. Sorting leaves into groups by their shape is... → identifying and classifying
    8. Who uses science at work? → vets, farmers, nurses and cooks
    9. You time a snail on wet ground, then on dry ground. Why use the same snail both times? → so only the ground changes, and the test is fair
    10. A toy robot moves and makes sounds. Why is it not a living thing? → it cannot feed, grow or make more robots by itself
    11. You want to find out if dogs with longer legs run faster. Which type of enquiry is that? → pattern seeking: look at lots of dogs for a pattern

Do the experiment for real

  • Does a plant need warmth?. Two small plants, watered the same. Keep one on a warm windowsill and one in the fridge. Compare them after five days.
  • Warm plant, cold plant. You need: Two small plants in pots, water, a warm bright windowsill, a cold but bright place, a grown-up. A grown-up finds a cold, bright place, such as a shady window in the coldest room. If there is none, skip this one. Never use a fridge, because it is dark inside. Water both plants the same. Put one on the warm windowsill and one in the cold, bright place. Each day, look at both and draw them. After five days, put them side by side and compare. Look for: Same water, same light, only colder. If the cold place is cold enough, that plant stops growing and droops. Say the conclusion out loud.
  • Celery drinks. You need: A stick of celery with leaves, a clear plastic cup of water, food colouring, a grown-up. Put a few drops of food colouring in the water. Stand the celery in it overnight. A grown-up cuts the stem across. Look at the cut end. Look for: Coloured dots in the stem: the tubes that carry water up to the leaves.
  • Find the four parts. You need: A weed from a garden, a park edge or a pot, with a grown-up who says it is safe to pull up. Pull up the weed the grown-up chose, gently, roots and all. Lay it out and find the roots, the stem, the leaves and a flower if it has one. Say what each part does. Wash your hands afterwards. Look for: The roots are the hairy part. Are they longer than you expected?

Answer keys

  • Does a plant need warmth? prediction: It will stop growing and droop; what happened: The warm plant stayed healthy; the cold plant drooped and its leaves went yellow
  • Plant check
    1. Which part takes in water from the soil? → the roots
    2. Which part makes the plant's food? → the leaves
    3. Which part makes seeds? → the flower
    4. A plant has water and light but is kept somewhere very cold. What happens? → it droops and stops growing
    5. Which three things does a plant need to be healthy? → water, light and the right temperature
  • Show what you know
    1. The stem's job is to... → hold the plant up and carry water
    2. Why are leaves wide and flat? → to catch as much sunlight as they can
    3. What are the roots for? → anchoring the plant and taking in water
    4. In the cold-plant experiment, what was the one thing that changed? → the temperature
    5. You predicted the cold plant would droop, and it did. Did the results support your prediction? → yes
    6. What conclusion did you make? → plants need the right temperature to stay healthy
    7. Why did both plants get the same water and the same light? → so only the temperature changed, and the test was fair
    8. A plant in a dark cupboard, with water, will... → go pale and thin
    9. The flower's job is... → making seeds
    10. What would happen to a plant if its roots were cut off? → it could not take in water, so it would droop
    11. You want to test if a plant needs light. Which is the fair test? → two plants with the same water and warmth, one in the light and one in the dark

Do the experiment for real

  • Minibeast groups. You need: A garden or park, a grown-up, a magnifying glass, paper. With a grown-up, find five small creatures under stones and leaves. A grown-up lifts each stone and puts it back. Look, do not touch. Wash your hands afterwards. Count the legs on each one. Say which are insects (six legs) and which are not. Look for: Woodlice have fourteen legs and spiders eight. Neither is an insect.
  • Group the pets. You need: Paper and a pencil. Write down every pet you know or have seen: at home, in your street, at a friend's or in a book. Next to each, write its group and the feature that tells you. Find one animal from a group nobody has as a pet. Look for: Most pets are mammals. Which groups are missing?
  • Draw a diagram. You need: Any animal picture, paper, a pencil, a ruler. Draw the animal simply. Draw a straight line from each part to the edge of the paper. Write the name of the part at the end of each line. Look for: A diagram names the parts. Did you label at least four?

Answer keys

  • Which group? Fish: salmon, shark; Amphibians: frog; Reptiles: snake, tortoise; Birds: eagle, penguin; Mammals: whale, bat; Insects: bee
  • Which group?
    1. Which group feeds its young milk? → mammals
    2. Which group has feathers? → birds
    3. How many legs does an adult insect have? → six
    4. Which group breathes through gills all its life? → fish
    5. A frog's smooth damp skin makes it a... → amphibian
    6. A snake's dry scales make it a... → reptile
  • Show what you know
    1. A bat flies. Which group is it? → mammal: it has fur and feeds its young milk
    2. A penguin swims. Which group is it? → bird: it has feathers and a beak
    3. Which group starts life in water and changes shape as it grows? → amphibians
    4. The three parts of an insect's body are... → head, thorax, abdomen
    5. A spider has eight legs. Is it an insect? → no, insects have six legs
    6. Where do most reptiles lay their eggs? → on land
    7. What is a diagram? → a drawing with labels naming the parts
    8. Where did you find the answers about newts and crocodiles? → in the fact card, a secondary source
    9. A dolphin lives in the sea and feeds its baby milk. Why is it not a fish? → feeding its young milk makes it a mammal
    10. A whale shark lives in the sea and breathes with gills all its life. Which group is it, and why? → a fish, because it breathes with gills all its life
    11. An animal has dry scales and lays eggs on land. Why is it not an amphibian? → amphibians have smooth damp skin and lay eggs in water

Do the experiment for real

  • Watch tadpoles. You need: A pond with frogspawn, when frogs lay eggs (spring, or the rainy season), a grown-up, a notebook. Look, do not take.. Visit the same pond every week. Stand back from the edge. A grown-up stays beside you. Draw what you see: spawn, tadpoles, legs, froglets. Write the date on each drawing. Look for: How many weeks from spawn to froglet?   • Caterpillar hunt. You need: Cabbages or other garden plants, a grown-up, a magnifying glass. With a grown-up, look under leaves for eggs, caterpillars or a chrysalis. Keep away from nettles: they sting. Do not touch hairy caterpillars. Draw what you find and leave it where it is. Come back in a week and look again. Wash your hands afterwards. Look for: Nibbled leaves are the clue. Which stage did you find?
  • Make a physical model. You need: Play dough or paper, a pencil. Make the four stages of a butterfly's life out of play dough: egg, caterpillar, chrysalis, butterfly. Put them in a circle with arrows drawn between them. Now draw the same circle on paper with labels. Look for: You made a physical model and a diagram of the same idea.

  • Diagram, or physical model? Diagram: a labelled drawing of the frog's life cycle, a map of the pond, a food chain drawn with arrows; Physical model: a plastic butterfly with wings that open, a globe, a toy skeleton

  • Show what you know

    6. Which takes longest to grow up? → a human
    7. A toy skeleton you can take apart is... → a physical model
    8. Why do scientists use models? → to show an idea clearly, leaving the rest out

    10. A tadpole has just grown its back legs. Predict what will happen next. → its front legs grow, then its tail shrinks
    11. Why is a physical model better than a diagram for showing how a skeleton's joints bend? → you can hold it and move the joints

Do the experiment for real

  • Feel your heart. You need: A clock with a seconds hand, a grown-up. Sit still and put two fingers on the side of your neck until you feel a beat. Count the beats for thirty seconds. Move as fast as you can on the spot for one minute, in any way you can. Then count again. Look for: Faster after moving: the heart pumps harder when your muscles need more.
  • Make the lung model. You need: A plastic bottle, two balloons, scissors, tape, a grown-up to cut. A grown-up cuts the bottom off the bottle. Hang one balloon inside from the neck and tape it. Stretch the other across the bottom and tape it. Pull the bottom balloon down, then let go. Look for: The inside balloon fills and empties. That is a physical model of a lung.
  • Draw a body diagram. You need: Big paper, a pencil, someone to lie down. Draw round a person lying on the paper. Draw the brain, lungs, heart, stomach and intestine where they go. Label each one with a line and a word. Look for: Is the heart in the middle of the chest? Is the intestine coiled below the stomach?

Answer keys

  • Organ check
    1. Which organ thinks and remembers? → the brain
    2. Which organ takes in air? → the lungs
    3. Food goes into a stretchy bag that churns it. Which organ? → the stomach
    4. Which organ is a long coiled tube? → the intestine
    5. The bottle-and-balloon lungs are which kind of model? → a physical model
  • Show what you know
    1. Where is your brain? → in your head
    2. What does the heart do? → pumps blood round the body
    3. Which organ is between the lungs? → the heart
    4. What happens to food in the stomach? → it is churned and broken down
    5. Where does the goodness from food go into your blood? → in the intestine
    6. In the lung model, what was the balloon inside the bottle? → a lung
    7. What is an organ? → a part inside the body with a job
    8. You put five labels on a body picture. What did you make? → a diagram
    9. What would happen in the lung model if you never pulled the bottom balloon down? → the lung balloon would stay empty
    10. What would happen if food could not get from the stomach to the intestine? → the goodness from food could not get into the blood
    11. Real lungs are not balloons. Why is the bottle-and-balloon model still useful? → it shows the idea: the breathing muscle pulls down and the lungs fill with air

Do the experiment for real

  • The Moon through a month. A clear night sky and a notebook. Draw the Moon's shape every night you can see it for four weeks, with the date.
  • Moon diary. You need: A clear night sky, a grown-up, a notebook, a pencil. With a grown-up, look at the Moon every night you can see it. Draw its shape and write the date. Keep going for about a month. Look at the row of drawings. Look for: New, crescent, half, full, and back. Observing over time.
  • A lamp and a ball. You need: A lamp in a dark room, a ball on a stick, a grown-up to set up the lamp. Stand with the lamp on one side. The lamp is the Sun; the ball is the Moon; you are the Earth. Hold the ball a little above your head and turn slowly on the spot. Watch how much of the lit side of the ball you can see. Look for: The ball is always a ball. You see a crescent, a half, a full, just like the Moon.
  • Day and night with a lamp. You need: A lamp in a dark room, a ball or an orange, a small sticker, a grown-up to set up the lamp. The lamp is the Sun and the ball is the Earth. Put the sticker on the ball: that is where you live. Hold the ball near the lamp, without touching the bulb, and turn it slowly. Watch the sticker move from the lit half into the dark half, and back. Look for: Half the ball is always lit and half is dark. As the Earth spins, you go from day into night. That is a physical model.
