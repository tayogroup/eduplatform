## Overview

Intensive English Level 2 takes a learner from a secure **A1** to a secure
**A2**: able to describe a routine, an experience and a past event in connected
sentences, say how long something has been true, explain how something works,
report what somebody said, give and take advice, say what will happen if
something else does, and write a short connected text with the parts joined and
punctuated.

It follows three Cambridge Primary English as a Second Language stages in
order. **Stage 4, Units 1 to 7, is A1 extending towards A2**: talking about your
own routine and your own past in more than one sentence, saying what you have
done and what you still have to do, and following a short set of instructions.
**Stage 5, Units 8 to 14, is A2 developing**: how long something has been true,
an opinion and a reason for it, how something works, and a longer story about
the past. **Stage 6, Units 15 to 20, is A2 consolidating**: keeping a
conversation going, passing on what somebody else said, describing how a thing
is made, giving advice, and writing a connected account of something that took
time.

Where Level 1 was organised by situation, Level 2 is organised by **what a
learner needs to say next**: the working week, offers and instructions, what you
have done, plans and predictions, an account of the past, quantities and
directions, comparing and obligation — then how long something has been true,
feelings and qualities, opinions, how things work, a longer past account,
arrangements and amounts — and finally conversation, processes, reported speech,
conditions, advice, and a whole account put together.

There is no Unit 0 and no alphabet work: the level opens at Unit 1 on the
learner's own week, and assumes the letters, the sounds and the Level 1 patterns
are already in place.

Every one of the **176 Cambridge 0057 objectives for Stages 4 to 6** is placed
in exactly one unit and cited by that unit's outcomes — 56 at Stage 4, 58 at
Stage 5 and 62 at Stage 6 — and every outcome also carries a CEFR level and one
of the five CEFR skills.

Across the level: **585 words**, **60 readings** (each unit has three — a short
text, an information text, and one real-world document: a notice, a form, a
timetable, a price list or a record card), **77 pattern cards**, **240 quiz
questions**, **192 comprehension questions**, **120 speaking tasks**, **80
writing tasks**, **120 practice activities** and **151 learning outcomes**, each
with an "I can" statement.

The level ends with the **A2 check**, in Unit 20, across the four skills,
scored and reported **separately for each skill**.

## Course policies

### Prerequisites

**Intensive English Level 1, or the equivalent.** Level 1 exits at a secure A1
and Level 2 opens at A1 extending towards A2, so the two levels now meet: a
learner who has finished Level 1 can begin Unit 1 with nothing in between.

> The earlier Level 2 opened at B1, two CEFR levels above where Level 1 ended,
> and its placement exam still tested a Level 1 that had been replaced. Both
> gaps are closed. The exam below was rewritten on 2026-09-12 against the real
> Level 1, and every unit it sends a learner back to is a unit that exists.

A learner arriving from outside takes the **Level 2 Placement Exam** first: 30
questions, 30 marks, about 30 minutes, in three sections — everyday words (11),
sentence patterns (10), and reading and talking to people (9). Attempts are
unlimited and it is never a fail; it decides only where you start.

It reports one of three answers. **Ready for Level 2** needs 80% overall and at
least 60% in every section. **Ready, with a little review** needs 50% overall,
and names the Level 1 units to warm up with. Below that it says so without
softening it: *"Your English will grow fastest on a strong foundation. Start
with Level 1 — it begins with the letters and their sounds and moves at your
pace, and this exam will be here whenever you are ready to try again."* The
everyday-words section is treated as critical: falling below 40% there points a
learner back to Level 1 whatever the overall score.

### Materials and equipment

- **A way to record audio.** Every one of the twenty assignments is a recording
  and a written piece, and nearly every speaking task ends with record, listen
  back and check.
- A device that plays audio, with headphones if possible. Every lesson, reading
  and speaking model has a Listen button.
- Paper and a pen, or a notebook. Several writing tasks and the capstone account
  are marked as handwritten work, on layout as well as language.
- A folder that keeps written work and recordings across the level. The capstone
  portfolio in Unit 20 is assembled from four earlier units.
- A notebook for vocabulary — 585 words, and from Stage 5 onward a growing share
  of them are abstract.
- An internet connection.

### Attendance

**This course has no live sessions.** It is written to be completed alone: every
exercise has a learner-facing answer key, every task that would need a partner
has a way to do it alone, and every pattern is shown worked through before the
learner tries it. A teacher, where there is one, is an accelerator and never a
dependency.

It is called *intensive*, and that word has to mean something the course cannot
supply for itself: {{how many hours a week a learner is expected to study, and
how that is checked}}.

### Homework

Every unit carries **one assignment**, **20 across the level**. Each is a
recording and a written piece — a card, a notice, a set of steps, a message, a
plan or an account. Personal details can always be made up; no task asks for a
real job, address, family or situation.

Alongside each assignment: a spoken lesson, three readings, nine or ten
comprehension questions, six speaking tasks, four writing tasks, three to five
pattern cards, six practice activities and a twelve-question quiz.

Units 7 and 14 are **stage checkpoints**: their quizzes and assignments revisit
the whole stage as well as their own unit. Unit 20 is the **Level 2 capstone**:
a portfolio taking one piece from four of Units 15 to 19, two new recordings, a
handwritten account of about 120 words, and the A2 check.

### Assessment and grading

**Assignments — 312 marks across the level.** An assignment is worth **4 marks
for each rubric criterion it is marked on**, so its marks and its rubric always
agree. Units 1 to 6 use three criteria (12 marks), Units 7 to 19 use four (16
marks), and the Unit 20 capstone uses all eight (32 marks).

The eight criteria, each marked 1 to 4, are shared by the whole course and are
the same eight as Level 1:

- **Speaking** — Pronunciation, Fluency, Interaction
- **Writing** — Accuracy, Range, Purpose
- **Listening** — Understanding
- **Reading** — Real-world documents

Writing: Accuracy is marked in all twenty units. Listening: Understanding and
Reading: Real-world documents are marked in the capstone alone, where the A2
check supplies the evidence for them.

> The earlier Level 2 awarded 25 or 30 marks per assignment against eight
> criteria worth 32, and no unit reconciled them. That question is closed at
> both levels in the same way: marks are now derived from the criteria each
> assignment names.

**Quizzes — 240 questions**, twelve per unit, each with an explanation of the
answer.

**Self-assessment — 151 "I can" statements**, carrying no marks.

**The A2 check** in Unit 20 has four parts, each scored **on its own**:
listening out of 9, reading out of 10, speaking out of 5 and writing out of 6.
The learner writes the four results as four lines and is told plainly not to add
them together — one number for four skills would hide the one that needs the
time — and then writes one sentence naming the skill to work on first. The
course data records passing this check as the entry condition for Level 3.
{{What counts as a pass, who if anyone confirms it, and what a learner who wants
a formal result is offered.}}

CEFR levels in this course are **teaching targets, not certified results**.
Finishing Level 2 does not by itself establish that a learner is at A2.

### Behaviour and participation

- **Record yourself, and listen back.** Almost every speaking task is built on
  it, because a learner working alone has no other way to hear their own
  mistakes.
- **Check with the key, then count.** Every practice says what a score means and
  where to go back if it is low.
- **Four lines, never one number.** The capstone formalises it; treat every
  quiz and practice the same way, by skill rather than by total.
- **Speak before you are ready.** Fluency and Interaction are two of the eight
  criteria; neither improves by waiting.
- **Keep everything.** The capstone portfolio is assembled from work done in
  Units 15 to 19.
