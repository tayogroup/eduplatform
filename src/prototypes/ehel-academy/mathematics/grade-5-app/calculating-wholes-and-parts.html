Calculating with Wholes and Parts

Rearrange a calculation to make it easier, multiply and divide numbers up to a thousand, add and subtract fractions once their denominators agree, take a unit fraction of a whole number, and keep the decimal point where it belongs when you add or multiply.

For the grown-up: what to have to hand

Squared paper for the multiplication grid, so each part lands in its own box. A strip of paper folded into halves, quarters and eighths is worth more than a drawing for the fraction steps - folding it again in front of the learner is what shows that dividing a half by three gives a sixth.

Support and challenge
An easier way in

Estimate first, every time, and write the estimate down before starting. Most of the mistakes in this lesson are a factor of ten out or a missing hundreds part, and both show up immediately against a rough answer you already committed to.

A harder challenge

Step 54 shows four calculations made easier by a law. Find three more of your own - one for each law - where rearranging saves real work, and write down the version somebody would do left to right so the saving is visible.

About

What this lesson is about

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Understand which law of arithmetic to apply to simplify calculations.
  • Estimate and multiply whole numbers up to 1000 by 1-digit or 2-digit whole numbers.
  • Estimate and divide whole numbers up to 1000 by 1-digit whole numbers.
  • Estimate, add and subtract fractions with the same denominator and denominators that are multiples of each other.
  • Estimate, multiply and divide unit fractions by a whole number.
  • Estimate, add and subtract numbers with the same number of decimal places.
  • Estimate and multiply numbers with one decimal place by 1-digit whole numbers.
Lecture

Unit lecture

The whole lesson, in three parts.

Part 1 of 3

Rearrange before you calculate

Addition and multiplication can be reordered and regrouped freely, so you can pick the easy pair first - 4 times 25 before the 17. Subtraction and division cannot, which is the limit worth knowing as well as the law.

Part 2 of 3

Break the number into parts

A three-digit multiplication is several easy ones added together, and a division is chunks of the divisor taken away until what is left is smaller than it. Both work because a number can be split into its hundreds, tens and ones.

Part 3 of 3

Make the pieces agree

Fractions can only be added once the pieces are the same size, and decimals can only be added once the points are under each other. Both are the same idea: line up what the digits or the pieces are worth before you start.

Step 54

Rearrange it to make it easier

Addition and multiplication can be done in any order, and grouped any way you like. That is not a trick — it is a law of arithmetic, and it lets you pick the easy pairing instead of working left to right.

Choose a calculation

Which law lets you do that?

Subtraction and division do NOT work this way. 20 − 5 − 3 is not 20 − 3 − 5 done in the other order and then reversed, and 100 ÷ 5 ÷ 2 is not 100 ÷ 2 ÷ 5 rearranged freely. The laws below belong to addition and multiplication only.
Step 55

Multiplying up to a thousand

Break the big number into its parts, multiply each part, then add. The grid keeps every part in its own box so nothing is lost.

Working

Estimate before you start. 346 × 7 is near 350 × 7, which is 2450. If your answer is nowhere near that, a part has gone missing from the grid — usually the hundreds.
Step 56

Dividing up to a thousand

Take away chunks you know. Each chunk is the divisor multiplied by something easy, and what is left at the end is the remainder.

What the remainder means

A remainder is not a decimal. 487 ÷ 6 is 81 remainder 1, and that 1 is one whole left over, not 0.1. Whether you write it as a remainder, a fraction or a decimal depends on what the question is about — one left-over person cannot be split.
Step 57

Adding fractions that agree

Fractions can only be added once the pieces are the same size. If one denominator divides into the other, change that one and the pieces agree.

Choose a pair

Working

Never add the denominators. 1/4 + 1/4 is 2/4, not 2/8. The denominator says how big each piece is, and adding two quarters does not make the pieces smaller — it makes you have more of them.
Step 58

A unit fraction of a whole number

A unit fraction has 1 on top. Multiplying a whole number by 1/n is the same as dividing it by n, and dividing a unit fraction by a whole number cuts each piece up further.

Choose one

Working

Dividing by a whole number makes a unit fraction SMALLER, not bigger. 1/2 ÷ 3 is 1/6, because each half is cut into three. Expecting division to make things smaller is right here — it is multiplying by a fraction that surprises people.
Step 59

Lining up the decimal point

Adding decimals is ordinary column addition, as long as the points are under each other. Choose a pair and see what happens when they are not.

Choose a calculation

Working

Fill the gap with a zero, do not leave it ragged. Writing 3.4 as 3.40 does not change its value and it puts a digit in every column. Lining the numbers up on the right instead of on the point is what turns 3.4 + 0.25 into a wrong answer.
Step 60

Multiplying a decimal

Multiply as though the point were not there, then put it back. The number of decimal places in the answer is the same as in the number you started with.

Choose a calculation

Check it against an estimate

The estimate is what catches a misplaced point. 3.6 × 4 is about 4 × 4 = 16, so an answer of 1.44 or 144 is wrong by a factor of ten either way. Rounding first costs a second and finds the commonest mistake in this whole lesson.
