Twenty-nine things to take away

A square number is a square of dots4 × 4 = 16, written 4² and said "four squared". Learn them to 12² = 144.
Squares grow by odd numbers1, 4, 9, 16, 25: the gaps are 3, 5, 7, 9. Each L you add is the next odd number.
Gaps not the same? Look at their gaps1, 4, 9, 16 has gaps 3, 5, 7, and the gaps of those are all 2: a square sequence. Cubes keep growing.
Odd numbers: start at 1, add 2The nth one is 2n − 1, so the 12th is 23, and 31 sits 16th because (31 + 1) ÷ 2 = 16. The first n of them add up to n².
Even numbers: start at 2, add 2The nth one is just 2n, and 98 sits 49th because 98 ÷ 2 = 49. The first n of them add up to n × (n + 1): a rectangle, always n more than the odds' square.
Factors stop, multiples do notA factor divides into the number exactly, and the factors come in pairs that meet at the square root — which is why 36 has nine of them and not ten. A multiple is the times table, and it never ends.
Highest common factor, lowest common multipleThe common factors are the overlap of two factor lists, and there is a highest because both lists stop. The common multiples never stop, so the one worth naming is the lowest. Check either with HCF × LCM = the two numbers multiplied.
Divisibility rules read only part of the numberThe last digit settles 2, 5 and 10; the last two settle 4 and the last three settle 8; the digits added up settle 3 and 9; and 6 is 2 and 3 together. There is no useful rule for 7 — divide.
Percentages: a fraction with its bottom number agreed in advancePer cent means for each hundred, so 65% = 65/100 = 0.65 — one number, three costumes. Find 10% by dividing by 10 and 1% by dividing by 100, then build any percentage out of those two. 100% is the whole and you can go past it.
Fractions: the bottom number counts cutsMore cuts means smaller pieces, so 1/4 is less than 1/3. Multiplying top and bottom by the same number changes the name and not the amount (3/4 = 6/8 = 75/100), and putting two fractions over the same bottom number is how you compare them.
Decimals: longer is not biggerEach column after the point is a tenth of the one before. Compare from the LEFT and stop at the first column that differs — 0.7 beats 0.65 in the tenths. A zero at the end changes nothing (0.2 = 0.20), a zero in the middle changes everything (3.04 is not 3.4).
Below zero: the minus says which side, not how bigThe line runs as far left of zero as right, and further left always means lower — so −7 is colder than −3, and any negative is below any positive. Zero is neither. Crossing zero is counted in two parts: 5 down to −3 is 5 + 3 = 8.
Rounding: read the next digit alongIt works the same on a decimal — 3.5 to the nearest whole is 4, read off the tenths digit. To round to the nearest hundred, look at the tens digit and nothing else: 5 or more goes up, less than 5 goes down. Round in one move from the original number — 149 is 100 to the nearest hundred, not 200. Exactly halfway goes up by agreement, not by arithmetic.
Comparing: =, > and <The sign opens towards the bigger number, so −7 < −3, 0.7 > 0.65, 1/3 > 1/4 and 68% > 66%. Every comparison in this lesson can be written down that way.
Two shapes, two factsAdd the two facts and one shape cancels out, leaving the other doubled: from ▲ + ● = 10 and ▲ − ● = 4 you get ▲ + ▲ = 14, so ▲ = 7. Two facts about two unknowns are exactly enough.
× 10 moves the digits, not the pointEvery digit shifts one column left for each 10, and right when you divide. "Add a zero" only ever described whole numbers: 3.5 × 10 is 35, not 3.50.
Ratio is part to part; proportion is part to whole3 : 2 means five counters shared three and two, so the proportion orange is 3/5 = 60%. A ratio is never a fraction, and only a proportion becomes a percentage.
× and ÷ before + and −3 + 4 × 5 is 23, not 35. Equal ranks go left to right, so 10 − 2 − 3 is 5. It is an agreement rather than a discovery, and brackets are how you overrule it.
Prime numbers: exactly two factors1 and itself, nothing else — and more than two factors makes a number composite, while 1 has one factor and is neither. There is no rule for them, so you sieve: cross out the multiples of 2, 3, 5 and 7, and the 25 primes up to 100 are what is left standing. 1 is not prime, 2 is the only even prime, and no square past 1 is ever prime.
Is it square? Try the nearest square50 is not: 7² = 49 leaves one dot over, and 8² = 64 is too many.
A square root undoes a square√49 = 7 because 7 × 7 = 49. √50 is not whole: it lies between 7 and 8.
A cube number is a square, stacked3³ = 3 × 3 × 3 = 27: three layers of 3² = 9. Cubes grow much faster than squares.
A cube root undoes a cube∛64 = 4 because 4 × 4 × 4 = 64, while √64 = 8. Try a few edges: there are only six cubes up to 216.
Triangular numbers count rows1, 3, 6, 10: rows of 1, 2, 3, 4 dots. Two triangles make a rectangle, so it is rows × (rows + 1) ÷ 2. Two in a row make a square: 6 + 10 = 16.
A sequence needs a start and a rule"Start at 5, add 4" gives 5, 9, 13, 17. Each term comes from the one before.
Find the rule from the gaps30, 26, 22: the gap is 4 every time, so the rule is "subtract 4". Check EVERY gap.
Shape patterns are sequencesCount pattern 1, 2, 3, write the counts as a sequence, and the gap is the rule.
Hops stay the same past zero4, 1, −2, −5: subtracting 3 keeps going left. Steps can be decimals too: 2, 1.5, 1, 0.5.
Count hops, not termsThe 8th term is the start plus 7 hops: the rule is used one time fewer than the number of terms.

Where each step sits in Cambridge Primary Mathematics 0096

This lesson deliberately runs past Stage 5 in places, and the badge on each step says where it is. Twenty of the twenty-eight are Stage 5 or below. Four are ahead of it. Cube numbers and common factors are Stage 6 objectives. square roots and cube roots appear nowhere in the primary framework at all, which is why their rows carry no code. Three more are on-stage steps that reach into Stage 6 near the end. They are the divisibility tests for 3, 6 and 9, the thousandths column, and taking a percentage of a quantity rather than of a shape. None of that is a mistake to be trimmed. Squares without square roots, and factors without common factors, would each be half a story, and a class that is ready can go on. It is only worth knowing which is which before you plan a lesson around it.
