Replace a Broken Tile

0

What this lesson is about

Take one broken tile out of a finished, grouted wall and put a new one back flush with its neighbours — masking and drilling to control the break, raking the joints first, chipping from the centre out, clearing the bed flat, then bedding, spacing, grouting and polishing.

By the end of this lesson you can:

  1. Name the tools and the protective equipment the job needs, and what each protects against.
  2. Mask and drill the tile to control where it breaks, to the depth of the tile only.
  3. Rake the grout from all four joints before anything is broken, and say why first.
  4. Chip the tile out from the centre outwards without damaging its neighbours.
  5. Clear the old adhesive back to a flat bed with the chisel bevel side down.
  6. Dry-fit the replacement, and read a rocking tile as an uneven bed.
  7. Butter the tile sparingly and set it flush on all four edges.
  8. Space it, leave it the stated curing time, then grout, finish and polish.

What the job needs

Assessed here

Tap each tool. Two of these are not tools at all, and they are the two that are not optional.

What goes wrong hereTreating goggles as advice. A glazed tile struck with a chisel does not crumble, it SPLINTERS, and it splinters upwards towards the face of whoever is swinging. The gloves are for afterwards: a freshly broken tile edge cuts like glass because it is glass.

3

Before the first blow

Assessed here

You are about to take out a tile in a finished bathroom wall. Tick every precaution.

What goes wrong hereRaking the grout after breaking the tile instead of before. Set grout ties all four edges of the tile to its neighbours. Strike it while that rim is intact and the crack does not stop at the joint — it runs through into the tile beside it, and a one-tile repair becomes a three-tile one.

4

The order of the job

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereStarting the chisel at the edge, where the tile looks weakest. The edge is where the NEIGHBOUR is. Struck there, the tile levers against the one beside it and the force goes into a tile you were not paid to replace. Start in the middle, where the only thing behind the tile is wall.

5

Taking the old tile out

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereLevering with the chisel instead of striking it. A lever needs a fulcrum, and the only fulcrum available is the edge of the neighbouring tile. Tiles are strong in compression and weak in bending, which is exactly the wrong way round for levering. Strike, do not prise.

Why it is done that way

Assessed here · and at the bench

Three that decide whether the repair works. Take your time.

What goes wrong hereMixing or opening the adhesive before the dry fit. Once it is on the tile you are working to the adhesive's timetable, not yours. Every check that can be made dry should be made dry.

7

Setting the new tile

Practised here · bench sign-off

What goes wrong hereGrouting before the adhesive has cured. The adhesive holds the tile; the grout only fills the joint. Grout a tile that is still moving and you lock it at whatever angle it happens to be sitting, and the joint cracks as it finally settles. Read the container — usually 24 hours — and do something else until then.

8

The words of this job

Assessed here

Eight words you will hear on any tiling job.

What goes wrong hereUsing 'grout' and 'adhesive' as if they were the same thing. The adhesive is behind the tile and holds it on. The grout is between the tiles and keeps water out of the joint. They are different materials, applied at different times, and a tile stuck on with grout will come off the wall.

✓

End of the lesson

You can plan a tile replacement, take the old one out without touching its neighbours and set the new one flush. What none of this gives you is the hand for it: the weight of the blow, the feel of a tile that is still holding, and whether the finished joint reads as a repair from a metre away. That is signed off at a wall, by an assessor, and nowhere else.
