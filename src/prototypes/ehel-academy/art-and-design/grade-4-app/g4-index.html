Grade 4 Art & Design

Eight lessons, in the order they are meant to be done. Every one is about making and looking: paints to mix, marks to make, patterns to build, art from far away to look at, and a journal to keep. About 335 minutes in all, one lesson a week in two short sittings - and a screen cannot be paint, so every lesson sends you to make something real at home.

What Stage 4 Art & Design covers

ExperiencingLooking at a street and finding why the far end is small; looking closely at a cloth printed from a carved block, a jar painted in bands, a cast metal head and a wall painting from long ago; and asking what each artist used and who they made it for.
MakingDrawing what is really in front of us, with guide lines and measured sizes; mixing a colour until it matches; printing in two colours that line up; building a pot from coils and slabs; making a model stand up; and adding pen, paper and marks on top of a painting.
ReflectingJudging our work against what it was for; giving a friend praise AND one piece of guidance; saying how another artist's work moved our own work on; and hanging an exhibition so other people can read it.

Make it for real

  • A street going away. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • Near or far?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Guide lines first. Give the child a pencil, a brush, a sponge and a piece of chalk. Ask for a straight line, a wavy line, a zigzag and lots of dots with each one. Which tool made the thickest mark?
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • A street from your window. You need: Paper, an HB pencil and a soft pencil or charcoal. Find a road, a corridor or a row of chairs going away from you. Mark the spot where the edges would meet, then draw the edges towards it. Draw the nearest thing biggest and darkest. Look for: Where was your vanishing point?
  • Pencil measuring. You need: A pencil, paper, and something to draw, like a mug or a shoe. Hold the pencil out at arm's length and measure the height with your thumb. Now measure the width the same way. How many widths make the height? Draw it at that proportion. Look for: Was it wider or taller than you expected?
  • Near, middle, far. You need: Paper and one pencil. Draw the same tree three times: big at the bottom, middle-sized higher up, tiny near the middle. Give the near one bark, leaves and branches. Give the far one no detail at all. Look for: Which one looks furthest away, and why?

Answer keys

  • A street going away find: The road is wide down here, close to you, and narrows as it goes away. The edges are straight lines heading for one spot.; The lamp nearest you is the tallest, and it stands lowest on the page. Each one further away is shorter, and stands higher up.; The house closest to you is the biggest, and it has the most detail: three windows, a door, a handle and a letterbox. The far one is a shape with one window.; Every line going away meets here, on the horizon: the level of your own eyes. That is the vanishing point.. Where do the two edges of the road meet? → at one spot on the horizon, level with your eyes
  • Near or far? Near: a tree drawn big, at the bottom of the page, a wall where you can count every brick, a door with its handle, its hinges and its letterbox, the edge drawn darkest of all; Far: a tree drawn small, near the middle of the page, hills drawn pale, with no detail at all, houses drawn as simple grey shapes
  • Guide lines first the page judges the stroke itself: a pale guide line with the pencil; the horizon with the pencil; the edge of the road with the pencil; the nearest edge, darkest with the charcoal. Then a free drawing: no key
  • How to draw what you see look carefully at the real thing → measure sizes with your pencil → draw light guide lines → check one shape against another → draw the real lines → put in the darks last
  • Distance spotter
    1. Lines going away from you meet at… → the vanishing point, on the horizon
    2. Far things are drawn… → smaller, paler and higher up
    3. What is a guide line? → a light line that helps you place things
    4. How do you compare sizes while you draw? → hold your pencil out at arm's length and measure
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: made a pale guide line with the pencil; made the horizon with the pencil; made the edge of the road with the pencil; made the nearest edge, darkest with the charcoal
  • Show what you know
    1. The same lamp post, further away, looks… → smaller
    2. Why draw the nearest edge darkest? → because dark edges come forward, so it reads as nearest
    3. Where is the vanishing point in a street drawing? → on the horizon, where the lines going away meet
    4. Why start with light guide lines? → because you can still change them
    5. Which shows something is far away? → pale, small and hardly any detail
    6. What do you draw first? → the biggest shapes, after looking
    7. Holding a pencil out at arm's length is for… → comparing sizes
    8. A tree drawn big at the bottom of the page reads as… → near

Make it for real

  • Mix it to match. Put out red, yellow and blue paint and a little white. Before each mix, ask: what do you think it will make? Mix it, look, and say what happened.
  • Light to dark. Mix a little black or white into one colour, four times, and paint four patches. Cut them out and put them in order from light to dark.
  • What was added?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • The right paint for the job. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • Nearly right. Look at something the child made. Ask: if you made it again, what one thing would you change? Then, if you can, change it together.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Match three things. You need: Paints, a brush, water, paper, and three things from your home. Pick a leaf, a stone and something from the kitchen. Mix a patch of colour beside each one until they match. Write beside each patch what you mixed. Look for: Which was hardest to match, and why?
  • A tone ladder. You need: One colour of paint, white paint, a brush and paper. Paint a patch of the colour straight from the pot. Add a little white, paint the next patch, and keep going for five patches. Line them up light to dark. Look for: Which step is closest to the sky today?
  • Calm it down. You need: Two opposite colours of paint, like red and green. Paint a bright patch of one colour. Mix in a tiny amount of its opposite and paint the next patch. Keep going until the colour is nearly brown. Look for: Where did it stop looking bright and start looking real?

Answer keys

  • Mix it to match
    • red + yellow → orange
    • blue + yellow → green
    • red + green → brown
    • black + white → grey
    then the child mixes any two they like: no key, the bowl shows what they made
  • Light to dark light to dark, by the colours' own lightness: almost white blue → very pale blue → light blue → mid blue → full blue
  • What was added? Added white: red became a soft pink, blue became a pale sky blue, orange became a pale peach; Added the opposite: bright red became a dull brown, bright green became a quiet green-brown, no lighter and no darker; Added black: blue became a deep midnight blue, green became a dark forest green
  • The right paint for the job
    • a soft wash of sky, with the paper glowing through (needs see-through) → Watercolour
    • covering a mistake you have already painted (needs covers) → Thick poster paint
    • white stars that stay white when you paint the sky over them (needs waxy) → Oil pastel
    • thin dark lines on top of dry paint (needs fine line) → Ink and a fine pen
  • Nearly right
    • Zain's brick wall: His red is far brighter than any brick he can see. → Add a little green, the opposite of red
    • Lina's misty hill: The far hill is as dark as the wall beside her. → Add white until it is pale
    • Ravi's lemon: His yellow has too much red in it, so the lemon looks like an orange. → Add more yellow
  • Colour spotter
    1. What does white do to a colour? → makes a lighter tint
    2. Which paint is see-through? → watercolour
    3. Red and green mixed make… → a dull brown
    4. A row of tints from light to dark is a… → tone ladder
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: mixed red and yellow and made orange; mixed blue and yellow and made green; mixed red and green and made brown; mixed black and white and made grey; put 5 colours in order from light to dark; chose Watercolour for a soft wash of sky, with the paper glowing through; chose Thick poster paint for covering a mistake you have already painted; chose Oil pastel for white stars that stay white when you paint the sky over them; chose Ink and a fine pen for thin dark lines on top of dry paint; fixed Zain's brick wall: add a little green, the opposite of red; fixed Lina's misty hill: add white until it is pale; fixed Ravi's lemon: add more yellow
  • Show what you know
    1. Blue and yellow make… → green
    2. Why add a little green to a red that is too bright? → because a little of the opposite colour makes it duller without darkening it
    3. Black and white make… → grey
    4. Which paint would keep white stars white under a dark wash? → oil pastel, because wax resists paint
    5. Why is a far hill painted paler? → because distance makes colours paler, so pale reads as far away
    6. Adding black to a colour makes… → a darker shade
    7. Your mix is nearly right. What should you do? → change one thing and check it against the real colour again
    8. What is the point of matching a colour? → so your picture looks like the thing you are painting

Where this art comes from

  • The printed cloth is drawn in the manner of the hand block printing of Rajasthan, in north-west India, where printers press blocks of teak or sheesham wood, carved by hand, onto cloth again and again - one block and one colour at a time, so a two-colour cloth is printed twice and the second block has to line up with the first. Whole families of printers work at it, and towns such as Bagru and Sanganer are known for their own colours and patterns. The flower on the page is our own, not a copy of any printer's block.

Make it for real

  • A cloth printed with a block. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • Build the repeat. Make a pattern with things from the kitchen: spoon, fork, spoon, fork. Ask what comes next. Then let the child make one for you to continue.
  • What to print with, and on. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • The print went wrong. Look at something the child made. Ask: if you made it again, what one thing would you change? Then, if you can, change it together.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • A foam block. You need: A clean polystyrene food tray, a blunt pencil, paint, a roller or brush, and paper. Cut a flat piece from the tray. Press your design into it with a blunt pencil, lines pushed well in. Roll paint over it and press it onto paper. Look for: Which lines printed, and which disappeared?
  • Print it twice. You need: Your block, a second block, paint in two colours, paper and a pencil. Print the lighter colour first and let it dry. Mark two small crosses on the paper, and two on the block. Print the second colour, lining the crosses up. Look for: How close did the two colours land?
  • A repeat of your own. You need: Your block, paint and a long strip of paper. Print your block in a straight row. Print a second row, shifted across by half a block. Keep going until the strip is full. Look for: Can you see where one press ends and the next begins?

Answer keys

  • A cloth printed with a block find: One flower is one press of the carved block. The printer inks it, presses, lifts, and moves along.; This row does not sit under the one above. It steps across by half. A shifted repeat hides the joins.; The red was printed first. The dark blue block went on top, and the red still shows at the edge.; A border block runs all the way round, printed after the middle is finished.. How was the red made to show under the blue? → the red was printed first, and the blue block went on top
  • Build the repeat
    • Flower, leaf, flower, leaf. What comes next? → flower, leaf
    • Flower, dot, dot. What comes next? → flower, dot, dot
    • Flower, leaf, dot, diamond. What comes next? → flower, leaf, dot, diamond
    then the child's own pattern: any row that repeats a unit of two or three tiles counts
  • What to print with, and on
    • printing a tile for the class cloth, which will be washed (needs stays in cloth) → Fabric ink
    • an ink you can clean off your block at the end of the lesson (needs washes off) → Water-based ink
    • a block you can draw your design into with a pencil (needs takes a drawn line) → A foam block
    • a test print where every edge must show clearly (needs crisp print) → Smooth paper
  • From block to class cloth design it in your journal → cut or press the block → take a test print → change the block → print your tile on the fabric → join the tiles into one cloth
  • The print went wrong
    • Ama's smudged flower: The flower is a solid blob and the cut lines have filled in. → Roll the ink out thinner before you ink the block
    • Hugo's two colours: The blue block printed half a centimetre to the left of the red. → Mark two register crosses and line the block up on them
    • Sofia's faint tile: Half the flower printed and half of it did not. → Print on a soft pad, and press evenly all over
  • Print spotter
    1. What are register marks for? → lining up the second block with the first
    2. Why wait before printing the second colour? → because wet ink under a new block makes mud
    3. A collaborative cloth is… → one piece made from everybody's tiles
    4. Filled-in cut lines mean… → too much ink
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: made a pattern that repeats; chose Fabric ink for printing a tile for the class cloth, which will be washed; chose Water-based ink for an ink you can clean off your block at the end of the lesson; chose A foam block for a block you can draw your design into with a pencil; chose Smooth paper for a test print where every edge must show clearly; fixed Ama's smudged flower: roll the ink out thinner before you ink the block; fixed Hugo's two colours: mark two register crosses and line the block up on them; fixed Sofia's faint tile: print on a soft pad, and press evenly all over
  • Show what you know
    1. How many blocks does a two-colour print need? → two, one for each colour
    2. Why take a test print before printing your tile? → because a mistake is easy to fix on the test and hard to fix on the cloth
    3. Hand block printers in Rajasthan press their design… → one block at a time, by hand
    4. What is a repeat? → the shortest bit of a pattern that happens again and again
    5. Why does a class cloth need fabric ink? → because the cloth will be washed and fabric ink stays in
    6. Your blue printed to the left of your red. What fixes it? → register marks
    7. A shifted row in a pattern… → steps across so the joins do not line up
    8. Your tile joins everyone else's. That makes it… → a collaborative work

Where this art comes from

  • The jar is drawn in the manner of ancient Greek black-figure pottery, made around 2,500 years ago, where the potter threw or coiled the vessel and a painter put the figures on in a slip that fired black, then scratched the details through it. Jars like this held oil, water or wine, and many carry scenes from stories that the people who used them knew well. The shape and the figures on the page are our own drawing in that manner, not a copy of a real jar.

Make it for real

  • How was it made?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.

  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • A coil pot. You need: Air-dry clay or salt dough, a blunt knife, water, and a board. Cut a round base. Roll coils as thick as your finger and score, slip and smooth each one on. Turn the pot as you build, and keep the rim level. Look for: Can you feel the coils inside?

Where this art comes from

  • The cast head is drawn in the manner of the brass heads made in Benin City, in what is now Nigeria, by the guild of casters who worked for the Oba's court. They were cast by the lost-wax method, which means the first model was made in wax, covered in clay, then melted out so the metal could take its place, and each head is a portrait of a ruler kept in the palace. Many of these works, the Benin Bronzes, were taken by a British force in 1897 and are now in museums far from Benin City, and some have been returned; whose they are is still being decided. The head on the page is our own drawing in that manner and copies no real head.
  • The wall painting is drawn in the manner of ancient Egyptian tomb and temple painting, made over three thousand years ago, where a figure is drawn so that every part is shown from its clearest side: the head and legs from the side, the eye and the shoulders from the front. Real paintings carry hieroglyphs, which are writing; the band of signs on the page is our own pattern and says nothing, because writing that means nothing should not be put in a child's mouth as though it did.

Where this art comes from

  • The stitched cloth is drawn in the manner of kantha from Bengal, in Bangladesh and the Indian state of West Bengal, where women stitch layers of old saris and cloth together with running stitch to make quilts and wraps, often with fish, flowers, birds and everyday scenes. Each kantha is made by its own maker and is often passed down in a family. The design on the page is our own, not a copy of a real kantha.
  • The printed cloth is drawn in the manner of the hand block printing of Rajasthan, in north-west India, where printers press blocks of teak or sheesham wood, carved by hand, onto cloth again and again - one block and one colour at a time, so a two-colour cloth is printed twice and the second block has to line up with the first. Whole families of printers work at it, and towns such as Bagru and Sanganer are known for their own colours and patterns. The flower on the page is our own, not a copy of any printer's block.
  • The jar is drawn in the manner of ancient Greek black-figure pottery, made around 2,500 years ago, where the potter threw or coiled the vessel and a painter put the figures on in a slip that fired black, then scratched the details through it. Jars like this held oil, water or wine, and many carry scenes from stories that the people who used them knew well. The shape and the figures on the page are our own drawing in that manner, not a copy of a real jar.

Make it for real

  • A gallery wall. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • Does it belong in this show?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Praise and guidance. Look at a drawing the child did not make. Ask them to say one kind thing about it that is really there in the picture.
  • Before the doors open. Look at something the child made. Ask: if you made it again, what one thing would you change? Then, if you can, change it together.
  • My journal, the whole year. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • A show at home. You need: Three pieces of your own work, paper for labels, and a wall or a table. Pick a theme, then pick the three works that fit it. Write a label for each: title, materials, your name, the date. Hang or stand them at the eye height of whoever will look. Look for: What did you leave out, and why?
  • Praise and guidance. You need: Someone else's work, and a sticky note. Look for a long time before you say anything. Write one true thing that works, naming what is really there. Write one thing to try next. Look for: Which half was harder to write?
  • The whole year. You need: Your journal or portfolio. Find the first thing you made this year and the last. Put them side by side. Write down three things you can do now that you could not do then. Look for: What do you want to get better at next year?

    1. Lines going away from you meet at… → the vanishing point
    2. To calm a colour that is too bright, add… → a little of its opposite
    3. Why does a two-colour print need register marks? → so the second block lands exactly on the first
    4. Why are the bottom coils of a tall pot thicker? → because they carry the weight above them
    5. Pen marks go… → on top of dry paint
    6. Naming what a work is made of means naming its… → medium
    7. Why plan the wall on paper before hanging a show? → because it is easier to move a plan than a hung picture
