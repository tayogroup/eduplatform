# ================================================================ Big Numbers and Below Zero
[big-numbers-below-zero.html] Numbers to 10,000
name: Ten hundreds make a thousand, so four places hold every number up to ten thousand.
show: 3,406 is 3 thousands, 4 hundreds, no tens and 6 ones. Press plus 1000 three times, plus 100 four times and plus 1 six times, and the blocks show 3,406.
warn: A zero is not nothing to skip. In 3,406 it holds the tens place, and without it the number would be 346.
hand: Build the number you are asked for, starting with the biggest blocks.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] What each digit is worth
name: A digit is worth a different amount depending on the column it sits in.
show: In 58 the 8 is worth 8. In 85 it is worth 80. In 80,000 it is worth eighty thousand. The digit is the same, and only its column has changed.
warn: Never read a digit on its own. Ask which column it is in before you say what it is worth.
hand: Point at the digit, name its column, then say what it is worth.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] The same number, regrouped
name: One thousand can be swapped for ten hundreds without changing the number. That swap is called regrouping.
show: 4,208 is 4 thousands, 2 hundreds and 8 ones. Swap one thousand for ten hundreds and it becomes 3 thousands, 12 hundreds and 8 ones. It is still 4,208.
warn: Regrouping does not make the number bigger or smaller. It only changes how the number is stored.
hand: Write the number again with one fewer thousand and ten more hundreds.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] Ten times, a hundred times
name: Multiplying by ten moves every digit one place to the left. Multiplying by a hundred moves it two places.
show: 34 times 10. The 3 tens become 3 hundreds and the 4 ones become 4 tens, so the answer is 340. 34 times 100 is 3,400.
warn: Adding a zero is not the reason it works. The digits move, and the zero only fills the empty ones place.
hand: Watch each digit move one column to the left, then read the new number.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] Counting on in steps
name: A sequence that counts on in equal steps can start from any number.
show: 1,240, then 1,290, then 1,340. Take one term from the next: 1,290 take away 1,240 is 50. So the next term is 1,340 add 50, which is 1,390.
warn: Find the step from two terms that sit next to each other. Guessing it from the first number alone goes wrong.
hand: Take one term away from the next to find the step, then use it to fill the gap.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] Below zero
name: Numbers can go below zero. We call them negative numbers, and a thermometer shows them.
show: It is 3 degrees and it gets 5 degrees colder. Count down 5 steps: 2, 1, 0, minus 1, minus 2. So it is now minus 2 degrees.
warn: Zero is a number you pass through, not a place where counting stops.
hand: Press colder one step at a time and say each number as you go.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] Below zero, in order
name: On a number line, further right always means bigger, even below zero.
show: Minus 2 is to the right of minus 9, so minus 2 is the bigger number. It is only 2 below zero, while minus 9 is 9 below.
warn: Minus 9 looks bigger because 9 is a big digit, but it is further left, so it is smaller.
hand: Picture the number line, then ask which number is further to the right.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] Smallest to largest
name: To put numbers in order, start with the one furthest left on the number line.
show: Minus 8, minus 3, 0, 5 is in order, smallest first. Minus 8 is furthest left and 5 is furthest right.
warn: Below zero, the bigger the digit looks, the smaller the number is. Minus 8 is smaller than minus 3.
hand: Find the smallest first, then keep choosing the next one to the right.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] Which one is it nearer?
name: Rounding is asking which neighbour a number is nearer to.
show: Round 4,630 to the nearest hundred. Its neighbours are 4,600 and 4,700. It is 30 past 4,600 and 70 short of 4,700, so it rounds to 4,600.
warn: Exactly halfway is the one case that needs a rule. 4,650 is halfway, and halfway rounds up, to 4,700.
hand: Find the two neighbours, then decide which one is nearer.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] One number, five answers
name: One number rounds to a different answer for each place you are asked about.
show: 47,318 to the nearest ten is 47,320. To the nearest hundred it is 47,300. To the nearest thousand it is 47,000.
warn: Always read which place the question asks for. Round it, on its own, is not a whole question.
hand: Say the place first, find the two neighbours for that place, then round.

[big-numbers-below-zero.html] Show what you know
name: This is the check for big numbers and numbers below zero.
show: You will read what digits are worth, round numbers, and put negative numbers in order. Get enough right to finish the lesson, and every answer tells you why it is right.
warn: If you fall short, press Try again. The questions come round again, and the reasons will help.
hand: For every number, name the column of each digit before you answer.

# ================================================================ Patterns and Square Numbers
[patterns-and-squares.html] Odd, even, and what happens
name: Pair up the counters. If one is left over, the number is odd.
show: 7 and 5 are both odd, so each has one left over. Put them together and the two leftovers make a pair, so 7 add 5 is 12, which is even.
warn: Adding two odd numbers never gives an odd number. The two leftovers always pair up.
hand: Choose two numbers, add them, and watch what happens to the leftovers.

[patterns-and-squares.html] Taking away, odd and even
name: Taking away follows the same rule as adding. Only the leftovers matter.
show: 9 take away 3. Both are odd, so each has one leftover. Taking one leftover away from the other leaves none, so the answer, 6, is even.
warn: An odd number take away an odd number is even, not odd, just as when you add them.
hand: Look at whether each number has a leftover, and predict before you work it out.

[patterns-and-squares.html] The shape that hides a number
name: A shape in a calculation stands for a number nobody has told you yet.
show: 15 add triangle is 22. The triangle must fill the gap from 15 up to 22, so the triangle is 7.
warn: The shape is not a guess. The rest of the calculation decides exactly what it is.
hand: Ask what the shape has to be for the calculation to be true.

[patterns-and-squares.html] When the shape comes first
name: When the shape comes first in a take away, it stands for the whole amount.
show: Triangle take away 14 is 30. Put the two parts back together: 30 add 14 is 44. So the triangle is 44.
warn: Do not take 14 away from 30. The triangle is the whole, so it has to be the biggest number.
hand: Put the two known parts back together to find the whole.

[patterns-and-squares.html] The same shape twice
name: A shape stands for the same number everywhere it appears.
show: Square add square add square is 24. Three equal parts make 24, so each square is 24 divided by 3, which is 8.
warn: Different shapes can stand for different numbers, but the same shape is always the same number.
hand: Count how many times the shape appears, then share the total equally.

[patterns-and-squares.html] What comes next?
name: Find the step from each term to the next. The same step every time is linear. A changing step is non-linear.
show: 5, 12, 19, 26. Every step is add 7, so it is linear and the next term is 33. 2, 5, 9, 14 goes up 3, then 4, then 5, so it is non-linear.
warn: Check every step, not just the first. A sequence can start like one kind and turn into the other.
hand: Work out the step between each pair of terms, then look at the steps.

[patterns-and-squares.html] Same step, or changing step?
name: This time the steps are not written in, so you work them out yourself.
show: 4, 8, 16, 32. The steps are add 4, add 8 and add 16. They change, so it is non-linear.
warn: Going up by a lot does not make a sequence non-linear. 100, 200, 300 goes up by 100 every time, so it is linear.
hand: Take each term away from the next one, and say whether the steps stay the same.

[patterns-and-squares.html] Say the rule
name: A term-to-term rule says how to get from any term to the next one.
show: 2, 5, 9, 14. The steps are add 3, add 4, add 5. The rule is: add one more each time than you added before.
warn: Add 3 fits only the first step. A rule has to work for every step, not just one.
hand: Say the rule, then use it to find the next term and check that it works.

[patterns-and-squares.html] Numbers that make squares
name: A square number is one whose dots make a perfect square.
show: A square with a side of 4 has 4 rows of 4 dots, which is 16. So 16 is a square number. The first four square numbers are 1, 4, 9 and 16.
warn: A square number is a number times itself, not a number times two. 4 times 4 is 16, not 8.
hand: Change the side and count the dots in the new square.

[patterns-and-squares.html] Between one square and the next
name: To grow a square by one each way, you add an L shape along two of its edges.
show: A 6 by 6 square becomes 7 by 7 when you add 6 down one side, 6 along the other and 1 in the corner. That is 13 dots.
warn: One new row is not enough. You need a new row, a new column, and the dot in the corner.
hand: Count the L before you count the whole square.

[patterns-and-squares.html] Show what you know
name: This is the check for odd and even numbers, missing numbers and sequences.
show: You will find hidden numbers, decide whether sequences are linear, and use square numbers. Get enough right to finish the lesson, and every answer tells you why it is right.
warn: If you fall short, press Try again. The questions come round again, and the reasons will help.
hand: In every sequence, work out the steps before you answer.

name: Nothing to work out here. This is your shelf, with a sticker for every step you finished.

# ================================================================ Ways to Calculate
[ways-to-calculate.html] Saying it in words
name: A big number reads itself once you split it into thousands, hundreds, tens and ones.
show: 7,405 is seven thousand, four hundred and five. The zero means there are no tens, so you say nothing for them.
warn: Do not say the zero out loud. We never say seven thousand four hundred and zero five.
hand: Split the number into its places, then say each part in turn.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Estimate before you work
name: An estimate is a rough answer you work out first, so that a silly answer stands out.
show: 387 add 214. Round them to 400 and 200. The answer should be about 600.
warn: An estimate is not the answer. You still work it out exactly afterwards.
hand: Round each number to something easy, then work with the easy numbers.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Adding it exactly
name: To add exactly, start with the ones. When a column makes ten or more, carry the ten into the next column.
show: 347 add 185. The ones make 12, so write 2 and carry a ten. The tens make 4 add 8 add 1, which is 13, so write 3 and carry a hundred. The hundreds make 5. The answer is 532.
warn: Forgetting a carried ten makes the answer too small. Write each carry above the next column so you cannot lose it.
hand: When you have an answer, check that it is close to your estimate.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Taking away exactly
name: To take away, when a column has too little, exchange one from the column next door.
show: 623 take away 187. 3 is less than 7, so exchange a ten, and 13 take away 7 is 6. The tens now hold 1, less than 8, so exchange a hundred, and 11 take away 8 is 3. Then 5 take away 1 is 4. The answer is 436.
warn: Never turn a column round and take the smaller digit from the bigger one. That gives 564, which is wrong.
hand: In each column, ask whether the top digit is big enough before you take away.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Tables, and a shortcut
name: In a chain of multiplications you may do the easy pair first.
show: 4 times 7 times 5. Do 4 times 5 first, which is 20. Then 20 times 7 is 140.
warn: This works for multiplying, but not for taking away or dividing.
hand: Look for a pair that makes 10, 20 or 100, and do that pair first.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Double one, halve the other
name: Doubling one number and halving the other leaves the answer the same.
show: 16 times 25. Halve 16 to get 8, and double 25 to get 50. 8 times 50 is 400, and so is 16 times 25.
warn: Doubling both numbers makes the answer four times too big. One goes up while the other goes down.
hand: Look for a number that is easy to halve, and double the other one.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Multiplying a big number
name: To multiply a big number, split it into parts, multiply each part, and add them back together.
show: 342 times 6. 300 times 6 is 1,800. 40 times 6 is 240. 2 times 6 is 12. Add them up and you get 2,052.
warn: Every part must be multiplied. Forgetting the middle part, the 40, leaves you 240 short.
hand: Write down the three parts first, then multiply each one.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Sharing out, and what is left
name: Dividing is taking away whole groups until you cannot take another. What is left is the remainder.
show: 87 divided by 5. 17 fives make 85, and 2 are left over. So 87 divided by 5 is 17 remainder 2.
warn: A remainder must be smaller than the number you divide by. If it is bigger, you could take another group.
hand: Check it: the groups times 5, add the remainder, should give 87 again.

[ways-to-calculate.html] 6s, 7s, 9s and factor pairs
name: Every rectangle you can make from a number of squares shows a factor pair.
show: 24 squares make a 4 by 6 rectangle, so 4 and 6 are a factor pair of 24. So are 3 and 8, 2 and 12, and 1 and 24.
warn: Do not stop at the first pair. Keep going until the pairs start to repeat.
hand: Pick a number and find every rectangle it makes.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Multiples and factors, both ways
name: Factor and multiple are one fact said from two ends.
show: 6 times 4 is 24. So 6 and 4 are factors of 24, and 24 is a multiple of 6 and of 4.
warn: The factor is the smaller number and the multiple is the bigger one. 24 is not a factor of 6.
hand: Say the fact as a multiplication first, then decide which end you are standing at.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Does it divide exactly?
name: You can tell whether a number divides exactly by looking at how it ends.
show: It divides by 2 if it ends in an even digit, by 5 if it ends in 0 or 5, and by 10 if it ends in 0. It divides by 25 if it ends in 00, 25, 50 or 75, and by 100 if it ends in 00.
warn: Having a 5 somewhere in the number is not the test. Only the end of the number counts.
hand: Look at the last two digits, then try each test in turn.

[ways-to-calculate.html] Show what you know
name: This is the check for calculating.
show: You will estimate, add, take away, multiply and divide, and use factors and multiples. Get enough right to finish the lesson, and every answer tells you why it is right.
warn: If you fall short, press Try again. The questions come round again, and the reasons will help.
hand: Estimate first, so you know roughly what the answer should be.

[ways-to-calculate.html] My stickers
name: Nothing to work out here. This is your shelf, with a sticker for every step you finished.

# ================================================================ Parts of a Whole
[parts-of-a-whole.html] More parts, smaller parts
name: The whole never changes size. Cut it into more equal parts and each part must get smaller.
show: A bar cut into 2 equal parts gives halves. Cut the same bar into 4 and each part is a quarter, half the size of a half.
warn: A bigger bottom number does not mean a bigger piece. One eighth is smaller than one quarter.
hand: Cut the bar into more parts and compare one part of each.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] A fraction is a division
name: Sharing is dividing, and a fraction is a way of writing the answer.
show: 3 cakes shared between 4 children. Each child gets 3 divided by 4, which is three quarters of a cake.
warn: The number of cakes goes on the top and the number of children on the bottom, not the other way round.
hand: Share the cakes, then say how much one child gets as a fraction.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] A fraction of an amount
name: A unit fraction tells you how many equal groups to make. One group is the answer.
show: One fifth of 20. Make 5 equal groups from 20, and each group has 4. So one fifth of 20 is 4.
warn: One fifth of 20 is not 5. The 5 says how many groups to make. The answer is how many are in one group.
hand: Divide by the bottom number to find one group.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] Fractions of amounts, in real life
name: A unit fraction of an amount means share the amount into that many equal groups. The answer is one group.
show: Amina has 30 mangoes and gives one fifth of them away. Share 30 into 5 equal groups, and each group holds 6. So she gives away 6 mangoes.
warn: The bottom number is how many groups, not the answer. One fifth of 30 is not 5, and it is not 25, which is what she keeps.
hand: Find the whole, divide it by the bottom number, and that is one part.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] Fractions in order
name: To put fractions in order, give them all the same bottom number first.
show: One quarter, one half and five eighths. In eighths they are two eighths, four eighths and five eighths. Now the tops say the order: one quarter, then one half, then five eighths.
warn: Do not order them by the bottom numbers. A bigger bottom number means smaller pieces, so one eighth is smaller than one half.
hand: Choose a bottom number that all the fractions can be changed into, then compare the tops.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] Same value, different name
name: Two fractions can look different and still be worth exactly the same.
show: One half and two quarters cover the same length of bar. So do one half and four eighths. They are equivalent fractions.
warn: Bigger numbers do not mean a bigger fraction. Four eighths is not more than one half.
hand: Line the two bars up and see whether they end in the same place.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] Out of a hundred
name: A percentage is how many parts in every hundred. Per cent means out of a hundred.
show: Shade 25 of the 100 squares and you have shaded 25 per cent. That is the same amount as one quarter of the grid.
warn: 25 per cent is not 25 things. It is 25 out of every hundred.
hand: Shade the squares you are asked for and read the percentage.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] Which is bigger?
name: To compare two fractions, give them the same bottom number first.
show: Three quarters or five eighths? Three quarters is the same as six eighths, and six eighths is more than five eighths. So three quarters is bigger.
warn: Do not compare the bottom numbers on their own. Eighths are smaller pieces, so you need to know how many of them there are.
hand: Change one fraction so both have the same bottom number, then compare the tops.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] Adding and taking away
name: When the bottom numbers match, the pieces are the same size, so you just count them.
show: Two fifths add one fifth is three fifths. Four sevenths take away one seventh is three sevenths.
warn: Never add the bottom numbers. The pieces are still fifths, so the bottom number stays 5.
hand: Estimate first, more or less than a whole, then count the pieces.

[parts-of-a-whole.html] Show what you know
name: This is the check for fractions and percentages.
show: You will find fractions of amounts, spot fractions that are worth the same, and compare them. Get enough right to finish the lesson, and every answer tells you why it is right.
warn: If you fall short, press Try again. The questions come round again, and the reasons will help.
hand: For every fraction, ask what one part is worth first.

name: Nothing to work out here. This is your shelf, with a sticker for every step you finished.

# ================================================================ Telling the Time
[telling-the-time.html] How long is that?
name: Every unit of time is made of a fixed number of smaller units.
show: 60 seconds make a minute, 60 minutes make an hour and 24 hours make a day. So 3 minutes is 3 lots of 60 seconds, which is 180 seconds.
warn: Time does not go in tens or hundreds. An hour is 60 minutes, not 100.
hand: Pick a unit and see how many of the smaller unit fit inside it.

[telling-the-time.html] Swapping units, both ways
name: To change to a smaller unit, multiply. To change back to a bigger unit, divide.
show: 4 hours is 4 times 60, which is 240 minutes. 180 minutes is 180 divided by 60, which is 3 hours.
warn: Going to a bigger unit always gives a smaller number, because each unit holds more.
hand: Ask whether you are going to a smaller or a bigger unit before you choose.

[telling-the-time.html] Three ways to say one time
name: One moment can be shown on a clock face, as a 12 hour time, or as a 24 hour time.
show: Twenty past three in the afternoon is 3:20 pm in 12 hour time, and 15:20 in 24 hour time.
warn: Without am or pm, a 12 hour time could be morning or afternoon. A 24 hour time never needs them.
hand: Press plus 1 hour a few times and watch all three change together.

[telling-the-time.html] Read the clock
name: The short hand gives the hour and the long hand gives the minutes, five for every number.
show: The short hand is between 4 and 5, and the long hand points at the 8. The hour is 4, the one it has passed, and 8 fives are 40. So it is 4:40.
warn: Read the hour the short hand has passed, not the one it is heading for.
hand: Find the hour first, then count the minutes in fives.

[telling-the-time.html] Writing it in 24-hour
name: In 24 hour time, morning hours stay the same and afternoon hours have 12 added.
show: 9:15 am is 09:15. 3:30 pm is 15:30, because 3 add 12 is 15. Midnight is 00:00 and midday is 12:00.
warn: 12:20 am is just after midnight, so it is 00:20. It is not 12:20, and it is never 24:20.
hand: Ask am or pm first, then decide whether to add 12.

[telling-the-time.html] Reading a timetable
name: A timetable reads down a column for one bus and across a row for one stop.
show: Follow the first bus down its column to see when it reaches each stop. Read along the library row to see when every bus gets there.
warn: Keep your finger on one row or one column. It is easy to slip onto the next line.
hand: Find your stop first, then read along its row.

[telling-the-time.html] Which one do you catch?
name: Catch the last bus that still gets you there in time.
show: Buses reach the library at 09:05, 09:25 and 09:45, and you must be there by 09:30. The 09:45 is too late, so you catch the one that arrives at 09:25.
warn: The earliest bus also gets you there, but you would wait for ages. The latest one that is still in time is the one to catch.
hand: Cross out the buses that arrive too late, then pick the latest one left.

[telling-the-time.html] How long between?
name: To find how long something lasted, count on from the start to the finish.
show: From 10:40 to 12:15. Count on 20 minutes to 11:00, then 1 hour to 12:00, then 15 minutes to 12:15. That is 1 hour and 35 minutes.
warn: Do not take the times away like ordinary numbers. An hour is 60 minutes, not 100.
hand: Count on to the next whole hour, then the whole hours, then the minutes left.

[telling-the-time.html] Over the end of the month
name: When you add days past the end of a month, you need to know how many days that month has.
show: 28 April add 9 days. April has 30 days, so 2 days take you to 30 April and 7 days are left over. That makes 7 May.
warn: There is no 37 April. Once you pass the last day of a month, you move into the next month.
hand: Use up the days left in this month first, then carry on into the next one.

[telling-the-time.html] Months and years apart
name: Months and years are counted the same way as hours and minutes.
show: From March 2024 to January 2026. Two whole years is 24 months, but January is two months before March, so it is 24 take away 2, which is 22 months.
warn: Count the whole years first and the leftover months after. Mixing them up gives the wrong answer.
hand: Count the whole years, then add or take away the extra months.

[telling-the-time.html] Show what you know
name: This is the check for time.
show: You will change units of time, read clocks and timetables, and work out how long things take. Get enough right to finish the lesson, and every answer tells you why it is right.
warn: If you fall short, press Try again. The questions come round again, and the reasons will help.
hand: Remember that an hour is 60 minutes whenever you count time.

name: Nothing to work out here. This is your shelf, with a sticker for every step you finished.

# ================================================================ Shape and Measures
[shape-and-measures.html] The faces of a solid
name: Every flat face of a solid is a flat shape you already know.
show: A cube has 6 faces, and every one is a square. A triangular prism has 2 triangle faces and 3 rectangle faces.
warn: A curved surface is not a flat face. A cylinder has only 2 flat faces, and both are circles.
hand: Pick a solid, take it apart, and name each face.

[shape-and-measures.html] Fold it up: nets
name: A net is a solid opened out flat.
show: Six squares joined in a cross fold up into a cube. Two circles and a rectangle roll up into a cylinder.
warn: Not every six squares make a cube. If two squares fold onto the same side, it does not work.
hand: Fold the net in your head, one face at a time.

[shape-and-measures.html] Every line of symmetry
name: A line of symmetry folds a shape into two halves that match exactly.
show: A square has 4 lines of symmetry: one down, one across, and the two diagonals. A rectangle has only 2.
warn: A rectangle folded along its diagonal does not match, because the corners do not land on each other.
hand: Test each line in turn until you have found all of them.

[shape-and-measures.html] Acute, right or obtuse?
name: A square corner is a right angle, 90 degrees. Smaller than that is acute. Bigger is obtuse.
show: An angle of 45 degrees is smaller than a square corner, so it is acute. An angle of 120 degrees is bigger than a right angle and smaller than a straight line, so it is obtuse.
warn: The length of the arms does not change the angle. Only the amount of turn between them matters.
hand: Turn the arm, and say acute, right or obtuse as it moves.

[shape-and-measures.html] Putting shapes together
name: Shapes can be joined to make new shapes, and some tile a floor with no gaps at all.
show: Two triangles join along their long side to make a square, and six triangles fitted round a point make a hexagon. Squares and hexagons tile a floor with no gaps, but circles always leave gaps.
warn: Shapes that tile must fit together at every corner, with no gaps and no overlaps.
hand: Pick two shapes, join them, and name the new shape they make.

[shape-and-measures.html] Area without counting
name: The area of a rectangle is the number of squares inside it, and there is a rule that finds it.
show: A rectangle 5 squares wide and 3 squares high has 3 rows of 5. That is 5 times 3, which is 15 squares.
warn: Do not add the width and the height. 5 add 3 is 8, but the rectangle holds 15 squares.
hand: Count the squares once, then check that width times height gives the same answer.

[shape-and-measures.html] All the way round
name: The perimeter is the distance all the way round the edge of a shape.
show: A garden 7 metres long and 3 metres wide. All the way round is 7 add 3 add 7 add 3, which is 20 metres. The short way is to add the length and the width and double it.
warn: Do not multiply the length by the width. 7 times 3 is 21, and that is the area inside, not the distance round.
hand: Add the length and the width, then double the answer.

[shape-and-measures.html] Two rectangles, one area
name: An L shape is two rectangles joined together, so its area is their two areas added.
show: A 5 by 3 rectangle is 15 squares, and a 2 by 2 square is 4 squares. Together the L shape covers 15 add 4, which is 19 squares.
warn: Do not treat the L as one big rectangle. The corner that is missing is not part of the shape, so its squares must not be counted.
hand: Find the area of each rectangle, then add the two areas.

[shape-and-measures.html] An odd shape on a grid
name: An odd shape has no rule, so you estimate its area by counting squares.
show: Count the whole squares inside the shape first. Then count each part square as about a half, so two part squares make about one whole square.
warn: Do not count a tiny sliver as a whole square. Two part squares together make about one.
hand: Count the whole squares, then pair up the part squares.

[shape-and-measures.html] Reading between the marks
name: When a pointer sits between two marks, work out how far across it is.
show: The marks say 200 and 300. The pointer is halfway between them, so it reads 250.

# ================================================================ Where Things Are
[where-things-are.html] Facing and turning
name: The four compass points go round in order: north, east, south and west.
show: Face north and make a quarter turn clockwise, and you face east. Another quarter turn and you face south.
warn: Clockwise goes north, east, south, west. Anticlockwise goes the other way: north, west, south, east.
hand: Say the four points in order, then make each turn one step at a time.

[where-things-are.html] The eight points
name: Between each pair of compass points sits another point, named by joining the two.
show: Between north and east is north-east, written NE. Between south and west is south-west, written SW.
warn: North or south always comes first. We say north-east, never east-north.
hand: Name the two points on either side, then join their names.

[where-things-are.html] Which way from here?
name: The eight compass points tell you which way to move.
show: Start in the middle and move one square north-west. You land on the square that is up and to the left.
warn: A move north-east goes up and to the right at the same time. It lands on the corner square, not the one above.
hand: Follow the direction, then tap the square you land on.

[where-things-are.html] Following a route
name: A route is several moves, done one after another.
show: From a square, go 3 east and then 2 north. You end up 3 squares to the right and 2 squares up from where you started.
warn: Do the moves in order. Only the east move changes the column, and only the north move changes the row.
hand: Do the first move, then the second, then tap the square you land on.

[where-things-are.html] Coordinates
name: Coordinates give the position of a point on a grid, along first and then up.
show: The point 4, 2 is 4 along the bottom and 2 up. Start at zero, go along 4, then go up 2.
warn: Always go along first. Going up first lands you on a different point.
hand: Start at zero, go along, then up, then tap the point.

[where-things-are.html] Along first, then up
name: In a pair of coordinates, the first number is along and the second is up.
show: 2, 6 is 2 along and 6 up. 6, 2 is 6 along and 2 up. They are two different places.
warn: Swapping the numbers moves the point. 2, 6 and 6, 2 are not the same place.
hand: Say along first, then up, every time you read a pair.

[where-things-are.html] The corners of a shape
name: Every corner of a shape on a grid has its own pair of coordinates.
show: A rectangle has corners at 1, 1 and 4, 3. Its other two corners are 4, 1 and 1, 3, because each corner takes its along from one corner and its up from the other.
warn: Read each corner for itself, along from the bottom and up from the side. Do not copy another corner.
hand: Put your finger on the circled corner and read along first, then up.

[where-things-are.html] Reflect it in the mirror
name: Every square of a shape has a partner the same distance away on the other side of the mirror line.
show: A square 3 away from the line on the left has its partner 3 away on the right. A square 1 away has its partner 1 away.
warn: A reflection flips the shape over the line. It does not slide it across unchanged.
hand: Take each square in turn, count to the line, and count the same on the other side.

[where-things-are.html] When the mirror is the edge
name: When the mirror line runs along the edge of a shape, the reflection touches the shape.
show: A square right against the mirror has no distance to cross, so its partner sits right beside it on the other side. The whole picture doubles.
warn: Do not leave a gap. There is no gap between the shape and the mirror, so there is none in the reflection.
hand: Start with the squares that touch the mirror, then do the rest.

warn: If you fall short, press Try again. The questions come round again, and the reasons will help.
hand: For every pair of coordinates, go along first and then up.

# ================================================================ Asking, Sorting and Chance
[asking-sorting-chance.html] What would answer it?
name: Before you collect any data, decide what would answer your question.
show: What colour do we like best is answered with groups you can name, like red or blue. How many brothers and sisters do we have is answered with numbers you count.
warn: Numbers cannot answer a question about names, and names cannot answer a question about numbers.
hand: Pick a question, then decide whether its answers are names or numbers.

[asking-sorting-chance.html] Tally, then count
name: A tally records answers one at a time, and every fifth mark crosses the other four like a gate.
show: 22 children answer. Four gates make 20, and 2 more marks make 22.
warn: Mark each answer as it comes. Trying to count them all at the end is how answers get lost.
hand: Press Next child and make one mark for each answer.

[asking-sorting-chance.html] One set, three pictures
name: The same data can be drawn in different ways, and each way is good at something different.
show: A bar chart makes it easy to compare groups at a glance. A pictogram uses a key, so one picture can stand for 2 children. A dot plot puts one dot for each answer.
warn: Read the key of a pictogram before you count. If one picture stands for 2 children, 4 pictures mean 8 children.
hand: Look at each picture in turn and say what it shows best.

[asking-sorting-chance.html] Sorting two ways at once
name: Venn and Carroll diagrams sort things by two questions at once.
show: Is it even, and is it more than 10? 12 is both, so it goes in the overlap of the two hoops. 7 is neither, so it goes outside both hoops.
warn: A number that answers yes to both questions belongs in the overlap, not in just one hoop.
hand: Ask both questions about the number, then put it in its place.

[asking-sorting-chance.html] Which chart would you choose?
name: Every chart is good at one job, so choose the one that does the job you need.
show: To count cars as they pass, use a tally chart. To see at a glance which fruit is most popular, use a bar chart. To sort by two questions at once, use a Carroll diagram or a Venn diagram.
warn: Any chart can show some data, but the wrong one hides the answer. A tally chart does not show at a glance which is biggest, and a bar chart cannot sort by two questions.
hand: Read what the child wants to show, then think which chart does exactly that.

[asking-sorting-chance.html] Maybe, likely or certain?
name: Impossible means it can never happen. Maybe means it might or might not. Likely means it will probably happen. Certain means it will happen.
show: Musa rolling a 7 on an ordinary dice is impossible. Hodan tossing heads is maybe. Picking a red sock from 9 red and 1 blue is likely. The sun rising tomorrow is certain.
warn: Likely is not the same as certain. Even when nearly all the socks are red, the blue one could still come out.
hand: Ask yourself, could it happen, and how often would it happen, then choose the word.

[asking-sorting-chance.html] Two classes, one question
name: Comparing two sets of data means looking for what is the same and what is different.
show: Classes 4A and 4B both have 22 children. In each class, 4 children come by car. But walking is most common in 4A, and the bus is most common in 4B.
warn: The same total does not mean the same answers. Look at every group, not just the total.
hand: Find one thing that is the same in both classes and one thing that is different.

[asking-sorting-chance.html] Impossible to certain
name: Some things cannot happen and some are sure to. Most sit somewhere in between.
show: Rolling a 7 on an ordinary dice is impossible. The sun rising tomorrow is certain. Rolling a 6 is possible but unlikely.
warn: Something unlikely can still happen. It just does not happen very often.
hand: Put each event on the line, from impossible to certain.

[asking-sorting-chance.html] Spin it, and spin it again
name: A chance experiment gives lumpy results after a few tries, and more even results after many.
show: The spinner has four equal parts, so each part should come up about a quarter of the time. After 10 spins one colour might come up 5 times. After 1000 spins each colour comes up close to 250 times.
warn: A few spins are not enough to tell whether a spinner is fair.
hand: Spin it 10 times, then 1000 times, and compare the counts.
