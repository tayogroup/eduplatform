Ehel Academy · Art & Design

Grade 2 Art & Design

Eight lessons, in the order they are meant to be done. Every one is about making and looking: paints to mix, marks to make, patterns to build, art from far away to look at, and a journal to keep. About 325 minutes in all, one lesson a week in two short sittings - and a screen cannot be paint, so every lesson sends you to make something real at home.

What Stage 2 Art & Design covers

ExperiencingPrinting with leaves, sponges and stamps; weaving over and under; walking outside and collecting things from nature; looking closely at printed cloth, a loom, a clay head from long ago and a paper cut; and recording what we find by drawing, sorting and moving.
MakingPrinting patterns that repeat; mixing tints and shades; weaving with wool, ribbon and paper; joining clay so it stays joined; tearing, cutting and sticking a collage; and choosing the right material, colour or tool for the job, a camera included.
ReflectingGrouping pictures by the feeling they give; saying what is the same in our work and an artist's; celebrating our friends' work with kind and true words; and looking back through a year of making.
Thinking and Working ArtisticallyHaving an idea and adding the thing that shows it; asking why two things would not join, and fixing it; trying new tools; and getting our work ready to show, together.

Where this art comes from

  • The stamped cloth is drawn in the manner of adinkra cloth from Ghana, printed by Asante makers with stamps carved from pieces of calabash (a hard gourd) and a dark dye made from tree bark, in squares ruled with a comb. Each real adinkra symbol has a name and a saying that goes with it. The page's stamps are simple shapes of our own and carry no meaning here: simple shapes can look like real symbols (the ringed circle is close to one), so the page uses them only to show how printing repeats a shape, not to stand for a meaning that belongs to someone else.

Make it for real

  • What will it print?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Pick the printer. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • Print a pattern. Make a pattern with things from the kitchen: spoon, fork, spoon, fork. Ask what comes next. Then let the child make one for you to continue.
  • A cloth printed with stamps. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Potato stamps. You need: Half a potato, a grown-up with a knife, paint on a saucer, and paper. Ask a grown-up to cut a simple shape into the flat side of the potato, like a star or a heart. Dab paint on the shape. Press it on the paper, then lift it straight up. Print it again and again in a row. Look for: Is every print the same? Which one came out best, and why?
  • Leaf prints. You need: Three different leaves, paint, a brush and paper. Paint the bumpy back of a leaf. Lay it paint side down on the paper and press all over it. Peel it off slowly. Try the other two leaves. Look for: Which leaf printed the most lines?
  • A printed border. You need: A bottle top, a sponge, a fork, paint and a big sheet of paper. Print a pattern all round the edge of the paper. Use two or three of the printers, in the same order every time. Draw a picture in the middle when the border is dry. Look for: Does your border repeat all the way round?

Answer keys

  • Things that print Which one prints lines, like the veins you can see on a leaf? → a leaf
  • What will it print? Lines: a fork, a leaf, a feather; Dots: bubble wrap, a sponge; A shape: a bottle top, a potato star, the end of a cardboard tube
  • Pick the printer
    • long grass (needs lines) → A leaf, A fork
    • the spots on a ladybird (needs dots) → A sponge, Bubble wrap
    • a sky full of stars (needs star) → A potato star
    • a row of balloons (needs circle) → A bottle top
  • Print a pattern
    • Leaf, star, leaf, star. What comes next? → leaf, star
    • Star, star, circle. What comes next? → star, star, circle
    • Hand, circle, leaf, circle. What comes next? → hand, circle, leaf, circle
    then the child's own pattern: any row that repeats a unit of two or three tiles counts
  • A cloth printed with stamps find: The same circle stamp, pressed four times in the square. Every one is the same, because it is one stamp.; These lines were drawn with a comb dipped in dye. The comb makes four lines at once.; A different stamp, a diamond, pressed again and again in this square.; A third stamp, a flower of dots. Three stamps, used over and over, fill the whole cloth.. How did the printer make the same shape again and again? → by pressing the same stamp again and again
  • Print spotter
    1. What do you do after you press the stamp down? → lift it straight up
    2. What does bubble wrap print? → rows of round dots
    3. Press the same stamp again, and you get… → the same mark again
    4. Which one prints a circle? → a bottle top
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: chose A leaf for long grass; chose A sponge for the spots on a ladybird; chose A potato star for a sky full of stars; chose A bottle top for a row of balloons; made a pattern that repeats
  • Show what you know
    1. What is a print? → a mark made by pressing
    2. Which one prints lines? → a leaf
    3. Why do all the circles on the printed cloth look the same? → because one stamp was pressed again and again
    4. Which one would you print a ladybird's spots with? → a sponge
    5. Hand, circle, leaf, circle. What comes next? → hand
    6. Why does a fork print lines? → because it has long, straight prongs
    7. Star, leaf, star, leaf, again and again, makes… → a pattern
    8. In Ghana, what are adinkra cloth stamps carved from? → a hard fruit shell

Make it for real

  • Tints and shades. Put out red, yellow and blue paint and a little white. Before each mix, ask: what do you think it will make? Mix it, look, and say what happened.
  • Red, from light to dark. Mix a little black or white into one colour, four times, and paint four patches. Cut them out and put them in order from light to dark.
  • How does it feel?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Colours for a feeling. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • Two paintings, two feelings. Put two pictures side by side (a book page and a photo will do). Ask: what do BOTH have? What does only one have?
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • One picture, two moods. You need: Paints, white and black paint, a brush, a saucer and two sheets of paper. Draw the same simple picture on both sheets: a house, a tree, or a boat on the sea. Paint the first one only in tints: mix a little white into every colour. Paint the second one only in shades: mix a tiny bit of black into every colour. Look for: Which one feels calm? Which one feels like night?
  • Mood hunt. You need: A grown-up, and some books or magazines with pictures. Find a picture that feels happy. Find one that feels calm, and one that feels stormy. Look at the colours in each one. Look for: What colours did the happy picture use? And the stormy one?
  • Paint a feeling. You need: Paints, a brush and paper. Think of a feeling: happy, calm, sleepy or excited. Choose three colours that give that feeling. Paint shapes and lines with only those colours. It does not have to be a thing. Look for: Can your grown-up guess the feeling from the colours?

Answer keys

  • Colours have feelings Which colours often feel calm and cool? → blue and green
  • Tints and shades
    • purple + white → light purple
    • green + black → dark green
    • yellow + white → light yellow
    • yellow + black → dark yellow
    • yellow + blue → green
    then the child mixes any two they like: no key, the bowl shows what they made
  • Red, from light to dark light to dark, by the colours' own lightness: pale pink → pink → red → dark red → deep red
  • How does it feel? Happy: a yellow sun over orange fields, a bright pink and orange party; Calm: a pale blue sea at dawn, soft green hills under a light blue sky, white snow with light blue shadows; Stormy: dark grey clouds and a zigzag of lightning, dark purple swirls and black trees
  • Colours for a feeling
    • a hot afternoon in the desert (needs warm) → Red, orange and yellow
    • a quiet swim in a cool lake (needs cool) → Blue, green and purple
    • a baby's soft blanket (needs pale) → Pink and light blue
    • a forest at night (needs dark) → Dark blue and dark green
  • Two paintings, two feelings both have: the sky; only one has: a sun, a moon, bright colours, mostly dark colours, stars. Which they like more: no key
  • Colour spotter
    1. A colour with white added is called… → a tint
    2. Green and black make… → dark green
    3. Which colours feel warm? → red, orange and yellow
    4. Dark grey clouds and lightning feel… → stormy
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: mixed purple and white and made light purple; mixed green and black and made dark green; mixed yellow and white and made light yellow; mixed yellow and black and made dark yellow; mixed yellow and blue and made green; put 5 colours in order from light to dark; chose Red, orange and yellow for a hot afternoon in the desert; chose Blue, green and purple for a quiet swim in a cool lake; chose Pink and light blue for a baby's soft blanket; chose Dark blue and dark green for a forest at night
  • Show what you know
    1. Purple and white make… → light purple
    2. A colour with black added is called… → a shade
    3. Why add only a tiny bit of black? → because a little black makes a colour much darker
    4. Which red is the lightest? → pale pink
    5. Which colours often feel calm? → pale blue and soft green
    6. Why paint a hot desert with warm colours? → because warm colours feel hot and sunny
    7. What do the sunny painting and the night painting both have? → the sky
    8. Grouping pictures by how they feel is a way of… → looking at and celebrating art

Where this art comes from

  • Weaving on a loom is done all over the world: kente in Ghana is woven on narrow strip looms, and in Guatemala and Peru weavers use a backstrap loom tied round the waist. The loom on the page is a simple card loom of the kind a child can make, with its threads named the way weavers name them: the warp is stretched on the loom, and the weft goes over and under it.

Make it for real

  • What to weave with. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • Fix the weave. Look at something the child made. Ask: if you made it again, what one thing would you change? Then, if you can, change it together.
  • A weaving on a loom. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Paper weave. You need: Two sheets of coloured paper, safe scissors, glue and a grown-up. Fold one sheet in half and cut slits from the fold, stopping before the edge. Open it out. Cut the other sheet into strips. Weave the first strip over, under. Weave the next one under, over. Push the strips close and glue down the ends. Look for: Does your weave make a pattern of squares?
  • Card loom. You need: A piece of card, safe scissors, wool and a grown-up. Ask a grown-up to cut small notches along the top and bottom of the card. Wrap the wool round and round through the notches. That is your warp. Weave a different wool over and under, across. Turn back at the edge and go the other way. Look for: Can you make stripes by changing the colour of the weft?
  • Weave hunt. You need: A grown-up and a walk around the house. Look for things that are woven: a basket, a mat, a scarf, a chair seat. Look closely. Can you see the over and under? Look for: How many woven things did you find?

Answer keys

  • Things people weave What is weaving? → going over and under, again and again
  • Make a paper weave, in order cut slits in a folded sheet, and cut some strips → weave the first strip over, under → weave the next strip under, over → push the strips close together → glue down the ends
  • What to weave with
    • a soft, warm scarf (needs soft) → Wool
    • a sparkly mat for a party (needs shiny) → Ribbon
    • a strong frame to weave on (needs strong) → Thin sticks
    • a mat to sit on in the rain (needs waterproof) → Strips of plastic bag
  • Fix the weave
    • Zara's paper weave: There are big gaps between the strips. → Push the strips close together
    • Kofi's wool weave: One piece of wool keeps falling out. → Weave it over and under, not just lying on top
    • Mei's ribbon mat: The strips slide out at the edges. → Glue down the ends
  • A weaving on a loom find: The warp threads are stretched tight from top to bottom. At the top, they are still waiting for the weft.; The weft goes across: over one warp thread and under the next. Look how each row goes the other way.; Two rows of red, two of yellow, two of blue, and again. The weaver made a pattern of stripes.; The loom holds the warp tight, so the weaving stays flat and even.. What goes over and under the warp threads? → the weft
  • Weave spotter
    1. The threads stretched tight on the loom are the… → warp
    2. After a row of over, under, the next row goes… → under, over
    3. Which would make a soft scarf? → wool
    4. What is a loom for? → holding the warp tight
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: chose Wool for a soft, warm scarf; chose Ribbon for a sparkly mat for a party; chose Thin sticks for a strong frame to weave on; chose Strips of plastic bag for a mat to sit on in the rain; fixed Zara's paper weave: push the strips close together; fixed Kofi's wool weave: weave it over and under, not just lying on top; fixed Mei's ribbon mat: glue down the ends
  • Show what you know
    1. Weaving is going… → over and under, again and again
    2. The thread that goes across, over and under, is the… → weft
    3. Why did Kofi's piece of wool fall out? → because it only lay on top instead of going over and under
    4. What do you do first for a paper weave? → cut slits in a folded sheet
    5. Why would you not weave a rain mat from paper? → because paper goes soggy when it gets wet
    6. Which would make a strong frame to weave on? → thin sticks
    7. How do you get rid of gaps in a weave? → push the strips close together
    8. Which of these is woven from dry grass? → a straw hat

Where this art comes from

Real Nok heads have carefully arranged hair and eyes shaped like half-moons with holes for the pupils. The picture is our own drawing in that manner, not a copy of a real head.

Where this art comes from

  • The stamped cloth is drawn in the manner of adinkra cloth from Ghana, printed by Asante makers with stamps carved from pieces of calabash (a hard gourd) and a dark dye made from tree bark, in squares ruled with a comb. Each real adinkra symbol has a name and a saying that goes with it. The page's stamps are simple shapes of our own and carry no meaning here: simple shapes can look like real symbols (the ringed circle is close to one), so the page uses them only to show how printing repeats a shape, not to stand for a meaning that belongs to someone else.
  • The tiled wall is drawn in the manner of the blue-and-white glazed tiles of Portugal (azulejos) and of İznik in Türkiye. Repeating star patterns are also at the heart of tile work across North Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia, such as the zellige of Morocco.
  • Weaving on a loom is done all over the world: kente in Ghana is woven on narrow strip looms, and in Guatemala and Peru weavers use a backstrap loom tied round the waist. The loom on the page is a simple card loom of the kind a child can make, with its threads named the way weavers name them: the warp is stretched on the loom, and the weft goes over and under it.
  • The clay head is drawn in the manner of the Nok terracotta sculptures of central Nigeria, made more than 2,000 years ago and among the oldest clay figures known in Africa south of the Sahara. Real Nok heads have carefully arranged hair and eyes shaped like half-moons with holes for the pupils. The picture is our own drawing in that manner, not a copy of a real head.
  • The paper cut is drawn in the manner of Chinese paper cutting (jianzhi), where red paper is folded and cut so that both halves come out the same. Similar cut-paper work is made in many places, such as the papel picado banners of Mexico. The design on the page is our own, not a copy of a real paper cut.

  • A home art corner. You need: A shelf or a table, your art from this year, and a grown-up. Choose four things you made this year. Put them on the shelf, grouped by how you made them. Add one thing from nature and one picture of art from far away, from a book or a card. Look for: Which work do you look at the most? Why?
  • Labels. You need: Small cards, a dark pen, and a grown-up to help with the writing. For each work, write what it is called, what you made it with, and your name. Put the label next to the work. Look for: Can a visitor tell how each work was made, just from its label?
  • Art corner tour. You need: Your home art corner and a visitor. Show a visitor round your art corner. For each work, say one thing you did and one thing you would change. Look for: What did your visitor like best?

    • A clay head → The rows of hair you pressed are so neat.

    1. A leaf print goes with… → the printed things
    2. What do the printed cloth and the tiled wall both have? → a repeating pattern
    3. A kind comment should name… → something really in the work
    4. A collage is curling up. What fixes it? → pressing it flat while it dries
  • Show what you know
    1. What is a print? → a mark made by pressing
    2. Blue and white make… → light blue
    3. In weaving, the thread that goes across is the… → weft
    4. Why do potters score and slip clay? → so the pieces stay joined when they dry
    5. Why do we group art in an art corner? → so people can see how the works connect
    6. Which line shows hopping forward? → a zigzag
    7. How did the paper cutter make two matching halves? → folded the paper before cutting
    8. What does a label tell a visitor? → what the work is, what it is made with and who made it
