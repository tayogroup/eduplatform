Repair a Dripping Tap

0

What this lesson is about

Tell a washer tap from a washerless one, isolate the supply and prove it, read which of the two leaks you have, strip the headwork without marking it, renew the washer — or the seat that keeps eating washers — repack a weeping stem, and put it back hand tight plus a half turn.

By the end of this lesson you can:

  1. Tell a washer (compression) tap from a washerless one, and say why it matters.
  2. Isolate the supply at the right point and prove it by running the tap dry.
  3. Strip the headwork in order without marking the finish.
  4. Tell a drip at the spout from a weep at the stem, and name the part at fault.
  5. Renew the washer and its brass screw, matched rather than guessed.
  6. Judge whether a seat can be renewed or must be dressed in place.
  7. Repack a weeping stem, wound the way the nut tightens.
  8. Reassemble greased, hand tight plus a half turn, and prove the repair.

Which kind is it?

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereBuying a washer before looking at the tap. The handle tells you at no cost. Several turns and increasing resistance is a washer tap. A quarter turn that stops dead, or a single lever, is not, and no washer in the shop will fit it.

Before the spanner

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereTrusting the valve without running the tap. Service valves seize part-open and stop taps under sinks are often not the one you think. Opening the tap and watching it die is the whole proof, and it is the step people skip because nothing appears to happen.

A tap in section

Assessed here · and at the bench

Tap each part. This is the same tap you have seen from the outside, cut in half.

What goes wrong hereCalling the whole assembly 'the tap'. The parts have names because the fault has a name. 'It drips' is not a diagnosis; 'the washer is grooved' and 'the seat is cut' are, and they get you the right part at the counter.

Which leak is it?

Assessed here

Three faults. Name the part before you name the tool.

What goes wrong hereFitting a washer to every leak. It is the cheapest part and the easiest to reach, which is why it gets fitted to faults it cannot cure. Two of the three above are not washer faults.

6

The order of the strip

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereTurning the stem the closing way to get it out. It threads out the way it opens. Turning it the other way drives the washer harder onto the seat, which is how a tight tap becomes a cut seat.

7

The washer, and the seat under it

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereDressing a seat that could have been unscrewed. Dressing removes brass, and a seat can only be dressed so many times. If it will come out, take it out and fit a new one — with a smear of sealant on the threads so the next person can get it out too.

Putting it back

Assessed here · and at the bench

Three that decide whether you come back to this tap.

What goes wrong hereTurning the water on at full bore. Open the valve slowly. A fault then shows as a weep you can see and stop, rather than as a jet behind a panel you cannot reach.

9

The words of this job

Assessed here

Nine words you will hear on any tap. Two of them are the two leaks.

What goes wrong hereUsing 'washer' for the seat, or 'packing' for the washer. They are three different parts sealing three different things: the washer seals the water off, the seat is what it seals against, and the packing stops water climbing the stem. Mix the words and you will be sold the wrong part.

End of the lesson

You can plan a tile replacement and a tap repair, take the old work out without damaging what stays, and read a fault before you reach for a part. What none of this gives you is the hand for it: the weight of the blow, the feel of a tile that is still holding, how far a gland nut goes past hand tight, and whether the finished work reads as a repair from a metre away. That is signed off on real work, by an assessor, and nowhere else.
