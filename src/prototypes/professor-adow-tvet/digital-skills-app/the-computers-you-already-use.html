The Computers You Already Use

0

What this lesson is about

Find the computer inside a meter, sort the machines in a workshop by whether they hold one, say what is lost when the digital half fails, share a workshop machine without losing someone else's work, and report a fault a technician can actually act on.

By the end of this lesson you can:

  1. Name the four things every computer does, and point to each on a real device.
  2. Find the computer inside equipment that is not called a computer.
  3. Say why a machine's controller cannot be used for something else.
  4. Separate a mechanical fault from a digital one.
  5. Say what data a device holds and who it belongs to.
  6. Start up, log in to and shut down a shared machine safely.
  7. Write a fault report a technician can act on.
Where this leadsThis module serves all six departments. Every trade in this school now works next to an embedded computer, and none of them calls it one. Where a criterion would map onto a Kenyan TVET unit it carries NO mapping rather than a guessed one: the Carpentry & Joinery Level 4 framework is the only one in hand, and digital skills are not in it. A claimed code that does not exist is worse than an honest blank.

The computer in a moisture meter

Assessed here

Tap each part. Every computer does these four things, and this one is in your hand.

What goes wrong hereCalling the display 'the meter'. The display is the last step, not the machine. A reading appears there because the probe measured something, a chip decided what the measurement meant for the material you selected, and a stored calibration table told it how. Blame the display for a wrong number and you will replace the one part that was working.

Which of these holds a computer?

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereSorting by 'does it have a screen'. The chiller controller and the meter both have screens; so does a microwave. The question is whether the machine can be given a different job. A screen is output — it tells you there is a computer, but no screen does not tell you there isn't one.

What the computer is actually doing

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereReaching for the multimeter before asking what the machine believes. An embedded machine acts on what it reads. Comparing what it THINKS is true against what you can measure yourself splits the whole problem in half in about a minute, and tells you which trade the job belongs to.

5

Sharing a workshop machine

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereUsing whichever account is already logged in, because it is quicker. It is quicker, once. Then your work is in their folder, their work has your changes in it, and when something is deleted there is no way to tell who did it. Shared machines are where records go missing, and a login is the only thing that makes the record honest.

6

Whose data is it?

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereAssuming data belongs to whoever typed it in. It almost never does. Work records belong to the employer and the customer, and that stays true when the work was yours, the reading was yours and the photograph is on your phone.

A fault report a technician can use

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereReporting a diagnosis instead of an observation. "The board has gone" tells a technician nothing they can check and sends them to one part. "It reads 0.0 on everything since it was dropped" sends them to the right place in a minute. Report what happened; let them say why.

End of the lesson

You can find the computer in a machine, get a device onto a network and say which part a fault is in. What none of this gives you is permission to open a live panel or reach into a stopped machine — that is signed off at the machine, by an assessor, and nowhere else.
