Hardware, Software and Machines That Act

0

What this lesson is about

Separate hardware from software and say which one a fault is in, understand why a machine behaves differently after an update, trace a signal from sensor to controller to actuator, and work safely beside a machine that can start without you.

By the end of this lesson you can:

  1. Separate hardware from software, and locate a fault in one or the other.
  2. Say what settings and firmware are, and why an update changes behaviour.
  3. Name the three parts of an automated machine and trace a signal through them.
  4. Tell a fixed sequence from a machine responding to a sensor.
  5. Work safely near an automated machine.
  6. Judge which tasks a machine does better than a person, and which it does not.
  7. Record a machine's settings before changing them, and restore them.
Where this leadsThis module serves all six departments. Every trade in this school now works next to an embedded computer, and none of them calls it one. Where a criterion would map onto a Kenyan TVET unit it carries NO mapping rather than a guessed one: the Carpentry & Joinery Level 4 framework is the only one in hand, and digital skills are not in it. A claimed code that does not exist is worse than an honest blank.

Hardware or software?

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereAssuming intermittent means electrical and consistent means software. It is a useful hunch and it is wrong about as often as it is right. A loose connection is intermittent; so is a setting that only applies after a restart. Ask instead whether the machine is doing what it was told. If it is, the instruction is wrong — that is software.

Settings, firmware and updates

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereTreating an update as maintenance that cannot do harm. An update is a replacement of the instructions the machine runs. It usually improves things and it is still a change, which is why it belongs on the job sheet next to the parts fitted.

A machine that acts on its own

Assessed here

Tap each part, and follow the signal left to right.

What goes wrong hereThinking of the controller as the dangerous part. The controller only decides. The actuator is the part with force behind it — the motor, the ram, the valve — and it moves when the controller says so, which may be while you are reaching past it.

Fixed sequence, or responding?

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereReading 'it has been quiet for a while' as 'it is not running'. For a sensor-driven machine, quiet is not a state. It is a gap between the last time the condition was met and the next.

Working near a machine that can start itself

Practised here · bench sign-off

What goes wrong hereTrusting the emergency stop as an isolator. It is the commonest and most serious confusion around automated plant. An e-stop is designed to halt a machine quickly during an emergency, which is a different job from keeping it halted while somebody's hands are inside it. Only an isolator, locked, with the key in your pocket, does the second.

What a machine is good at

Assessed here

What goes wrong hereJudging automation by whether it replaces a person. Most of it does not. It changes which part of the job the person does, and the part left over is usually the part that needed the training.

Before you change a setting

Assessed here · and at the bench

What goes wrong hereRemembering the old settings instead of writing them down. You will remember them for about an hour. The machine will be someone else's problem for years, and 'it used to be better before it was adjusted' with no record of what it was is a fault nobody can fix.

End of the lesson

You can find the computer in a machine, get a device onto a network and say which part a fault is in. What none of this gives you is permission to open a live panel or reach into a stopped machine — that is signed off at the machine, by an assessor, and nowhere else.
