Shapes and Angles

Sort triangles by their sides and their symmetry, measure and classify an angle by eye, use the 180° on a straight line to find one you cannot see, separate perimeter from area and watch one change while the other stays put, cut a compound shape into rectangles, fold a net into a cube, and finish a symmetrical pattern.

For the grown-up: what to have to hand

A ruler, and a sheet of squared paper for the perimeter and area work. The corner of any piece of paper is a ready-made right angle for testing whether an angle is acute or obtuse. For the nets, six paper squares taped edge to edge, or a small cardboard box cut open along its edges and laid flat - folding a real one is what makes an arrangement that cannot fold obvious.

Support and challenge
An easier way in

Start with the corner of a piece of paper as your right angle. Hold it against each angle in the lesson and say only whether the angle is smaller or bigger than the corner - acute or obtuse - before worrying about the number of degrees.

A harder challenge

Every rectangle in Step 32 has whole-number sides. Find the rectangle with perimeter 24 that has the LARGEST area, then try perimeter 20 and perimeter 36. What shape does the biggest-area rectangle always turn out to be, and why?

About

What this lesson is about

By the end of this lesson you will be able to…

  • Identify, describe, classify and sketch isosceles, equilateral or scalene triangles.
  • Estimate and measure perimeter and area of 2D shapes, understanding that shapes with the same perimeter can have different areas.
  • Draw compound shapes that can be divided into rectangles and squares, and estimate, measure and calculate their perimeter and area.
  • Identify, describe and sketch 3D shapes in different orientations.
  • Identify and sketch different nets for a cube.
  • Use knowledge of reflective symmetry to identify and complete symmetrical patterns.
  • Estimate, compare and classify angles, using geometric vocabulary including acute, right and obtuse.
  • Know that the sum of the angles on a straight line is 180 degrees, and use this to calculate missing angles.
Lecture

Unit lecture

The whole lesson, in three parts.

Part 1 of 3

Sorting by the sides

Triangles are named by how many sides are the same length - all three, exactly two, or none. The angles always agree with the sides, so an equilateral triangle has three equal angles and a scalene triangle has none.

Part 2 of 3

Naming an angle by eye

A right angle is 90 degrees, the corner of a page. Smaller than that is acute and larger is obtuse, so you can classify an angle by holding a corner against it without measuring anything.

Part 3 of 3

Round the edge, or inside

Perimeter is the distance all the way round and area is how many squares fit inside. They answer different questions, which is why two rectangles can use the same length of fence and hold different amounts of room.

Step 29

Three kinds of triangle

Triangles are sorted by how many of their sides are the same length. Pick a kind and look at what that does to the angles and to the lines of symmetry — the sides and the angles always agree with each other.

Choose a kind of triangle

What is true about it

Equal sides and equal angles come as a pair. Where two sides are the same length, the two angles opposite them are equal too. That is why a scalene triangle, with no two sides the same, also has no two angles the same — and why it has no line of symmetry at all.
Step 30

Acute, right or obtuse

A right angle is exactly 90°, the corner of a page. Everything smaller is acute, everything larger (up to a straight line) is obtuse. Drag the arm and name the angle before you read the label.

Your turn: what kind is it?

Estimate against the right angle. You do not need a protractor to classify one. Hold the corner of a page against it: if the arm falls inside the corner it is acute, if it falls outside it is obtuse.
Step 31

Angles on a straight line

Two angles that sit together on a straight line always add to 180°. That is enough to work out one you cannot measure, as long as you know the other.

Find the missing angle

Subtract from 180, not from 90. The commonest slip is to reach for 90 because a right angle is the angle you know best. On a straight line the total is 180°, so the missing angle is 180 minus the one you have.
Step 32

Perimeter is not area

Perimeter is the distance all the way round the edge. Area is how many squares fit inside. They measure different things, and changing a shape can move one while the other stays exactly where it was.

Build a rectangle

Every rectangle with perimeter 24

Same perimeter, different area. All of those rectangles use 24 cm of edge, and their areas run from 11 to 36 squares. So knowing the perimeter tells you nothing on its own about how much room is inside — the squarer the rectangle, the more area the same edge encloses.
Step 33

Cut it into rectangles

A compound shape is not a shape with its own formula — it is rectangles stuck together. Cut it into rectangles you can already do, work each one out, then add. For the perimeter, walk all the way round the outside instead.

Working

The cut line is not part of the perimeter. When you split the shape, the new edge down the middle belongs to both rectangles — it helps you find the AREA, but it is inside the shape, so it is never walked when you measure the distance round the outside.
Step 34

Solids and the nets that fold into them

A solid keeps its faces, edges and vertices however you turn it — a cube lying on a corner is still a cube. A net is that solid opened flat, and not every arrangement of six squares folds back up.

Choose a solid

Which of these fold into a cube?

Six squares is necessary, not sufficient. Every one of these has six squares. A net fails when two squares would land on the same face as it folds — so count the squares first, then check the arrangement.
Step 35

Finish the symmetrical pattern

A mirror line means every square has a partner the same distance away on the other side. Click the squares on the right to complete the reflection.

Where the mirror is

Count from the line, not from the edge. A square two columns from the mirror reflects to two columns from the mirror on the other side. Counting from the edge of the grid instead is what makes a pattern come out shifted.
