Grade 4 Global Perspectives

Eight lessons, in the order they are meant to be done. Every one is about finding things out and talking about them: a question to build, a classmate to ask, a picture to read, a problem to solve, a garden to plant together. About 350 minutes in all, one lesson a week.

Lesson 1

Where Food Comes From

A loaf and a banana are on the shelf, but where did they come from, and how far did they travel? Construct your own questions about the journey of our food, then locate the answers inside a text and a market.

16 steps · about 45 min · stickersStart Lesson 2

Waste Watchers

What does our school throw away? Observe and count the litter after lunch, ask the class what they do with an empty carton, read the rain gauge for the compost project, and record every finding in the right chart.

17 steps · about 50 min · stickersStart Lesson 3

Sleep and Screens

How much do we sleep, and what do we do in the evening? Survey the class, draw conclusions the data proves and no further, tell a conclusion from a guess, and sort our evening activities on a Venn diagram.

16 steps · about 45 min · stickersStart Lesson 4

The Old Field

A supermarket is planned for the old field at the edge of town. The corner shopkeeper, a mother of four, the farmer and the supermarket company all think differently. Work out who thinks what, find the author's viewpoint in a leaflet and a letter, and give your own opinion about somebody else's, with reasons.

16 steps · about 45 min · stickersStart Lesson 5

Shared Spaces

In a shared space, what you do for a small reason reaches other people. Find the cause behind an action and its consequence for others in the lunch queue, the library and the hall, then suggest actions of your own for problems that belong to somebody else.

15 steps · about 40 min · stickersStart Lesson 6

The Class Museum

A team gives out its jobs so each goes to the person who can do it. Allocate the jobs for the class museum about the old town and for the bee garden, bring ideas when the team is stuck, then look honestly at what you did well and what took more than one go.

15 steps · about 45 min · stickersStart Lesson 7

Energy Talks

A talk needs a start, a middle and an end. Build two talks with that structure, listen to three classmates present and respond with relevant ideas and questions, and construct questions to understand a talk better.

16 steps · about 45 min · stickersStart Lesson 8

Look Back

Seven lessons of investigating, thinking, weighing up, working together and presenting. Look back over all of them: what did you learn, how did your ideas change, and which kinds of activity helped you learn?

14 steps · about 35 min · stickersStart

What Stage 4 Global Perspectives covers

ResearchConstructing our own questions to understand a topic; locating the information and the answers inside a source; investigating by questionnaire, observation and measurement; and selecting, organising and recording what we found in charts and diagrams.
AnalysisKnowing that people think and believe different things about a topic; drawing conclusions from charts and numbers; talking about the causes of what we do and its consequences for others; and suggesting actions that could make a difference to an issue affecting other people.
EvaluationDiscussing a source and seeing that its author has a viewpoint; and giving an opinion about somebody else's viewpoint, with reasons.
ReflectionSaying what we did well in a team and what we could do better; saying how working together made the outcome better; saying what we learned and how our ideas changed; and saying which kinds of activity help us learn.
CollaborationGiving out the team's jobs so each goes to the right person; and bringing ideas to the team while working positively with everyone in it.
CommunicationPresenting information about a topic clearly, with a start, a middle and an end; and listening to others, then responding with relevant ideas and questions.

Do it together, for real

  • Construct your questions about food. Pick a topic (pets, the park, dinner). Take turns asking a question about it that starts with What, Where, Who, When, Why or How. Count how many different questions you can ask.
  • Locate the answers in the text. Read a short page of a non-fiction book together. Ask a question, and let the child find the ONE sentence that answers it and read it out.
  • Locate it in the market. Look at one photo or picture book page together. Ask: what does this picture tell us? Find three things in it and say a fact about each.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Where did it come from?. You need: A grown-up and three things from the kitchen cupboard. Read each label and find where the food came from. Construct one question about each one's journey that the label does not answer. Guess which travelled furthest, then check on a map. Look for: Which food had the longest journey? Did the label say how it travelled?
  • Locate the answer. You need: A leaflet, a recipe or a food label, and a grown-up. Your grown-up asks a question the text can answer. Locate the one sentence that answers it and read it out. Ask them one the text CANNOT answer, and say what source would. Look for: Was the answer where you expected it to be?
  • The market source. You need: A shop or market and a grown-up. Find one food grown near you and one from far away. Find something that tells you HOW it arrived: a box, a sign, a van. Tell your grown-up the four stages of one food's journey. Look for: Which stage could you not find out about in the shop?

    5. Where do bananas turn yellow? → In a warm room near the shops the bananas turn yellow, and then they are ready to sell.

Do it together, for real

  • Observe the playground after lunch. Stand at a window or a gate for five minutes and count one kind of thing - red cars, people with dogs, buses. Make a mark for each. Then count a second kind and compare.
  • What the litter count shows. Look at the picture rows you made. Ask: which row is the longest? How many chose this? Did anyone choose that?
  • Ask: what do you do with an empty carton?. Ask everyone at home one question with a few answers (how do you get to work or school?). Draw one small picture per person in a row for each answer.
  • Which bin does it belong in?. Draw two big circles or two columns on paper and sort what you found out into them, one fact at a time, saying why each goes where it goes.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Observe the bin. You need: A grown-up and the kitchen bin at the end of a day. Decide what counts: packets, peel, paper, plastic. Count one kind at a time. Draw a bar for each kind. Look for: Which kind is there most of? Could any of it have gone somewhere else?
  • Ask at home. You need: Everyone at home. Ask everyone the same question: what do you do with an empty tin? Give them three choices and record each answer. Make a table of the answers. Look for: Did everyone answer the same? What would change the answers?
  • Measure the rain. You need: A jar, a ruler and a week. Put a straight-sided jar outside where rain can fall in. Each morning measure the water in millimetres and write it down. At the end of the week, find the wettest day and the driest. Look for: How much more fell on the wettest day than the driest?

Lesson 4: The Old Field16 steps · about 45 minutes · 5 objectives

What it teaches (Cambridge Primary Global Perspectives 0838, Stage 4)

  • 4Ap.01 Recognise that people think or believe different things about a topic
  • 4Es.01 Discuss a source, recognising that the author has a clear viewpoint on the topic
  • 4Ea.01 Express an opinion about another person’s viewpoint, giving reasons for opinion
  • 4Fv.01 Talk about what has been learned during an activity and consider how personal ideas have changed
  • 4Fl.01 Identify which types of activities support learning

The steps

  1. What this lesson is about · What you will be able to do by the end.
  2. Unit lecture · Listen to the lesson, one part at a time. Press Next part when you are ready.
  3. Big words · Tap each word to hear what it means and how to use it. Then show you know them.
  4. One field, four views · Everybody knows the same facts about the old field. They still think different things. Press Next .
  5. Who thinks what? · Teacher Yasmin describes a view. Who holds it? Tap the person.
  6. A fact, or what somebody thinks? · Some of these are facts about the old field. Some are what a person THINKS should happen. Which is it?
  7. The company's leaflet · The supermarket company posted a leaflet through every door. Find the sentences that show what the AUTHOR thinks. Then say what the viewpoint is.
  8. The shopkeeper's letter · Mr Omar wrote a letter to the town newspaper. Find HIS viewpoint. Is it the same as the leaflet's?
  9. What do YOU think of their views? · Now you. Do you agree with Mr Omar? With Mrs Farah? With Mr Hassan? Say what you think of each view, with two reasons.
  10. Views and viewpoints · Think about who thinks what, the leaflet, the letter and your opinions. Tap the answer.
  11. Look back · What did you learn, how did your ideas change, and which kind of activity helped you learn? Tap to say it.
  12. Games · Choose a game to play. Nothing here is new.
  13. Find out at home · Finding out does not stop at school. Do one of these with a grown-up.
  14. Show what you know · Time to show what you know. Tap the answer.
  15. Our world · Where these skills are used out in the real world. This part is still being built.

Do it together, for real

  • Who thinks what?. Ask the child a question and listen for whether the answer is ABOUT the question. Swap: they ask, you answer with something off the point, and they catch you.
  • The company's leaflet. Read a short page of a non-fiction book together. Ask a question, and let the child find the ONE sentence that answers it and read it out.
  • What do YOU think of their views?. Ask: what do you think about ___? Listen for an opinion AND a reason, and make sure the reason is about the topic.
  • Look back. At the end of the day ask: what did you learn today? What did you like best, and why?
  • Who thinks what at home?. You need: Everyone at home and a topic people disagree about: bedtime, screens, what to have for dinner. Ask each person what they think should happen. Ask each person WHY, and write the reason beside their name. Say how each person's view comes from where they stand. Look for: Did anyone change their view after hearing the reasons?
  • Find the viewpoint. You need: A leaflet, an advert or a letter that came through the door, and a grown-up. Find who wrote it. Find the sentence that says what the author wants you to do. Find one reason the author gives. Look for: Would the author gain anything if you did what they asked?
  • My opinion, two reasons. You need: A grown-up. Pick one view from the old field: Mr Omar's, Mrs Farah's, Mr Hassan's or Dana's. Say whether you agree, partly agree or disagree. Give two reasons, both about the field. Look for: Could your grown-up give a reason on the other side?

Answer keys

  • Who thinks what?
    1. Who thinks the supermarket will close small shops? → Mr Omar, the corner shopkeeper
    2. Who thinks food will be cheaper and closer? → Mrs Farah, the mother of four
    3. Who thinks the field should stay a field for the sheep? → Mr Hassan, the farmer
    4. Who thinks the supermarket will bring eighty jobs? → Dana, from the supermarket company
  • A fact, or what somebody thinks? A fact: The old field is at the edge of town, Sheep have grazed the field for forty years, The nearest big shop is two bus rides away; What somebody thinks: The supermarket should not be built, Cheaper food matters more than one field, Eighty jobs are worth losing a field for
  • The company's leaflet
    1. Which sentence shows what the author WANTS you to do? → We hope you will support the plan at the meeting on Tuesday. Greenway Stores Ltd.
    2. Which sentence gives the author's reason about JOBS? → The store will bring eighty new jobs for local people.
    3. Which sentence tells you WHO the author is? → We hope you will support the plan at the meeting on Tuesday. Greenway Stores Ltd.
    4. What is the author's viewpoint? → The supermarket should be built on the old field
  • The shopkeeper's letter
    1. Which sentence says what the author thinks the town SHOULD do? → I think the town should say no to the plan and keep the field, and its small shops.
    2. Which sentence gives his evidence about the NEXT town? → When a supermarket opened in the next town, four of its small shops closed within a year.
    3. Which sentence gives a reason a SMALL shop is different? → A big supermarket cannot know your name or keep a loaf back for you on a Saturday.
