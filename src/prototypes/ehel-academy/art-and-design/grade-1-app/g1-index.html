Ehel Academy · Art & Design

Grade 1 Art & Design

Eight lessons, in the order they are meant to be done. Every one is about making and looking: paints to mix, marks to make, patterns to build, art from far away to look at, and a journal to keep. About 330 minutes in all, one lesson a week in two short sittings - and a screen cannot be paint, so every lesson sends you to make something real at home.

What Stage 1 Art & Design covers

ExperiencingMeeting lines, colours, textures, shapes and patterns; looking at art from long ago and far away; touching and trying paints, chalks, clay, scissors and glue; and gathering and recording what we find by sorting, ordering and mark making.
MakingUsing pencils, brushes, sponges, chalk and our own fingers with more and more confidence; mixing colours; joining and connecting things to make a model; and choosing the right material for what we want to make.
ReflectingCelebrating what we made and what our friends made; saying something kind and true about a picture; and finding what is the same and what is different in two works of art.
Thinking and Working ArtisticallyHaving an idea and making it into a picture; trying something new, like rice in the paint; and looking at what we made and saying what we would change next time.

Lesson 1: Marks and Lines15 steps · about 40 minutes, in two sittings · 6 objectives

What it teaches (Cambridge Primary Art & Design 0067, Stage 1)

  • 1E.01 Encounter, sense, experiment with and respond to a wide range of sources, including a range of art from different times and cultures.

  • 1R.01 Celebrate artistic experiences and learning.

Two sittings

The steps

  7. What kind of line? · Look at each thing. Is its line straight, wavy or zigzag?
  8. Lines on a cave wall · This is a painting from long, long ago, on the wall of a cave. Tap the things in it.
  9. Line spotter · Which one is it? Tap the answer.
  10. My journal · Look back at what you made today. Which did you make first? What would you change next time?

Where this art comes from

  • The cave wall is drawn in the manner of the painted caves of Europe, such as Lascaux in France (about 17,000 years ago) and Chauvet in France (more than 30,000 years ago), and of the hand stencils at the Cueva de las Manos in Argentina (about 9,000 years ago) and in the caves of Sulawesi in Indonesia, where some are more than 40,000 years old. People in many parts of the world made pictures like these long before writing. The picture on the page is our own drawing in that manner, not a copy of one wall.

Make it for real

  • Make the marks. Give the child a pencil, a brush, a sponge and a piece of chalk. Ask for a straight line, a wavy line, a zigzag and lots of dots with each one. Which tool made the thickest mark?
  • What kind of line?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Lines on a cave wall. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Five tools, one paper. You need: A big piece of paper, a pencil, a brush and some paint, a sponge, a piece of chalk, and your own finger. Draw a straight line with the pencil. Draw a wavy line with the brush. Dab with the sponge, draw with the chalk, and make dots with your finger. Look at all five marks together. Look for: Which tool made the thickest mark? Which made the thinnest?
  • Line hunt. You need: A grown-up and a walk around the house or outside. Find something with a straight line, like a door. Find something wavy, like a curtain or a puddle edge. Find something zigzag, like a roof or a fence. Look for: Which kind of line did you find most?
  • Rubbing lines. You need: Thin paper, a crayon with the paper peeled off, and a coin, a leaf or a bumpy wall. Put the paper over the coin or the leaf. Rub the side of the crayon over it, gently. Watch the lines appear. Look for: What kind of lines came through?

Answer keys

  • Kinds of line Which line goes up and down gently, like the sea? → wavy
  • Make the marks the page judges the stroke itself: a straight line with the pencil; a wavy line with the brush; a zigzag with the crayon; lots of dots with the finger; a long line with the chalk. Then a free drawing: no key
  • What kind of line? Straight: a road, a ladder; Wavy: a snake, a wave, a thread on the floor; Zigzag: mountain tops, lightning
  • Lines on a cave wall find: The artist drew the animal with thick, dark lines. Look at its legs and its back.; Someone put their hand on the wall and blew colour around it. That was their mark.; A row of dots, made one tap at a time, just like you did.. What did the cave artist use to make the animal? → thick, dark lines
  • Line spotter
    1. Which tool makes a wide, wet line? → a brush
    2. A line that turns sharply, like lightning, is… → zigzag
    3. A line that does not bend is… → straight
    4. Lots of little dots in a row make a… → dotted line
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: made a straight line with the pencil; made a wavy line with the brush; made a zigzag with the crayon; made lots of dots with the finger; made a long line with the chalk
  • Show what you know
    1. What is a tool? → a thing you make art with
    2. Which line goes up and down gently, like the sea? → wavy
    3. Which tool makes a soft, blobby dab? → a sponge
    4. A tree trunk line is… → thick
    5. Where did people paint thousands of years ago? → on cave walls
    6. Why does a brush make a thicker line than a pencil? → because its tip is wider
    7. Which line is made of sharp turns? → zigzag
    8. Why draw a calm sea with wavy lines, not zigzags? → because calm waves go gently up and down

Where this art comes from

  • The striped cloth is drawn in the manner of kente, the woven cloth of Ghana, made by Asante and Ewe weavers in narrow strips that are sewn together. In real kente the patterns and colours have names and meanings that the weavers give them, so the page's stripes are our own simple pattern and not a real kente design. A photograph of a real one is well worth finding together.

Make it for real

  • Mix two colours. Put out red, yellow and blue paint and a little white. Before each mix, ask: what do you think it will make? Mix it, look, and say what happened.
  • Light to dark. Mix a little black or white into one colour, four times, and paint four patches. Cut them out and put them in order from light to dark.
  • Warm or cool?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • Colours in a cloth. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Real mixing. You need: Red, yellow and blue paint, a little white, a paper plate and a brush. Put a blob of red and a blob of yellow next to each other. Say what you think they will make. Then mix them. Try red and blue, then blue and yellow, then red and white. Look for: Did each mix make what you said? Which one surprised you?
  • A tone ladder. You need: One colour of paint, some white, some black, and a strip of paper. Paint a patch of the colour in the middle of the strip. Add a little white and paint a patch to the left. Add more white, another patch. Add a little black and paint to the right. Then more black. Look for: Can you see the ladder from light to dark?
  • Warm and cool hunt. You need: A grown-up and a room full of things. Find five things with warm colours: red, orange or yellow. Find five things with cool colours: blue or green. Put them in two piles. Look for: Which pile makes you feel warmer just by looking?

Answer keys

  • The three primary colours Which of these is NOT a primary colour? → orange
  • Mix two colours
    • red + yellow → orange
    • red + blue → purple
    • blue + yellow → green
    • red + white → pink
    • blue + black → dark blue
    then the child mixes any two they like: no key, the bowl shows what they made
  • Light to dark light to dark, by the colours' own lightness: white → light blue → blue → dark blue → black
  • Warm or cool? Warm: the sun, a fire, a pumpkin, a tomato; Cool: an ice cube, the sea, blueberries
  • Colours in a cloth find: Red is a warm colour. The weaver put it at the top and near the bottom.; Yellow diamonds sit in the middle. Warm and bright, so your eye goes there first.; Green is a mix of blue and yellow. It is cool next to the red.; Black squares repeat along the stripe. Black is the darkest tone there is.. What does the weaver do with the colours in this cloth? → puts them in stripes that repeat
  • Colour quiz
    1. What happened when we mixed red and blue? → it made purple
    2. To make a colour lighter, you add… → white
    3. Blue and yellow make… → green
    4. Which colour is the darkest tone? → black
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: mixed red and yellow and made orange; mixed red and blue and made purple; mixed blue and yellow and made green; mixed red and white and made pink; mixed blue and black and made dark blue; put 5 colours in order from light to dark
  • Show what you know
    1. Which three are the primary colours? → red, yellow and blue
    2. Red and yellow make… → orange
    3. Red and blue make… → purple
    4. Red and white make… → pink
    5. Why does a little white make a colour lighter? → because white is the lightest colour of all
    6. Why paint a sunset in red and orange? → because red and orange feel warm, like the sun
    7. The weaver put the colours in… → stripes that repeat
    8. Before you mix, an artist should… → guess what it will make, then look
Lesson 3: Feel the Texture15 steps · about 40 minutes, in two sittings · 7 objectives

Make it for real

  • Rough, smooth or soft?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • What does it do to the paint?. Put a spoon of paint in four cups. Stir rice into one, flour into one, sugar into one, water into one. Predict first, then look and touch.
  • The right material for the job. Put out five scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt. Say what you want to make (a rough trunk, a fluffy cloud) and ask which scrap would work, and why.
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Texture paint. You need: Four cups with a spoon of paint in each, some rice, some flour, some sugar, some water, and paper. Stir rice into the first cup, flour into the second, sugar into the third, water into the fourth. Before each one, say what you think it will do. Paint a patch of each on the paper and touch it when it is dry. Look for: Which patch is bumpiest? Which is smoothest?
  • Rubbing hunt. You need: Thin paper and a crayon with the paper peeled off. Find five bumpy things: a coin, a leaf, a brick wall, the sole of a shoe, a basket. Make a rubbing of each one. Stick them in a row. Look for: Can a grown-up guess what each rubbing is?
  • A texture picture. You need: Card, glue, and scraps: sandpaper, cotton wool, foil, bubble wrap, felt, wool. Draw a simple picture: a tree, a cloud, a fish, the sea. For each part, choose the scrap that FEELS like it. Glue them on. Look for: Close your eyes and feel the picture. Can you tell what each part is?

Answer keys

  • Touch and feel Which word means how a thing FEELS? → texture
  • Rough, smooth or soft? Rough: sandpaper, a brick, tree bark; Smooth: a mirror, a spoon, an ice cube; Soft: a kitten, a pillow
  • What does it do to the paint?
    • add rice → bumpy
    • add flour → thick
    • add sugar → gritty
    • add water → runny
  • The right material for the job
    • a rough tree trunk (needs rough) → Sandpaper
    • a fluffy cloud (needs soft) → Cotton wool
    • a shiny fish (needs shiny) → Foil
    • a bumpy crocodile back (needs bumpy) → Bubble wrap
  • Texture quiz
    1. Texture is how a thing… → feels
    2. Which material would you choose for a fluffy cloud? → cotton wool
    3. What did rice do to the paint? → made it bumpy
    4. To make a rubbing, you rub the crayon… → on its side, gently
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: added rice to paint and made it bumpy; added flour to paint and made it thick; added sugar to paint and made it gritty; added water to paint and made it runny; chose Sandpaper for a rough tree trunk; chose Cotton wool for a fluffy cloud; chose Foil for a shiny fish; chose Bubble wrap for a bumpy crocodile back
  • Show what you know
    1. Which of these is rough? → tree bark
    2. Which of these is smooth? → a mirror
    3. What does flour do to paint? → makes it thick
    4. What does water do to paint? → makes it runny
    5. Why does a rubbing of a coin show the bumps? → because the crayon catches on the raised parts
    6. A rubbing shows… → a texture on paper
    7. Why put sandpaper on a picture of a tree trunk? → because a trunk is rough
    8. Why stick cotton wool on a picture of a cloud? → because a cloud looks soft and fluffy

  6. What comes next? · A pattern says the same thing again and again. Tap the tile that comes next. Then make your own.
  7. Patterns in tiles · This wall is made of tiles. Tap the shapes to find the pattern.
  8. Draw shapes and patterns · Now draw them yourself. Make the shape the card asks for.

Where this art comes from

  • The tiled wall is drawn in the manner of the blue-and-white glazed tiles of Portugal (azulejos) and of İznik in Türkiye. Repeating star patterns are also at the heart of tile work across North Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia, such as the zellige of Morocco.

Make it for real

  • Which shape is it?. Sort the pebbles, the buttons or the leaves: by colour, then by rough and smooth, then by big and small. Say why each one goes where it goes.
  • What comes next?. Make a pattern with things from the kitchen: spoon, fork, spoon, fork. Ask what comes next. Then let the child make one for you to continue.
  • Patterns in tiles. Find a picture of art from another country or from long ago (a library book or a museum website). Find three things in it and say what the artist did.
  • Draw shapes and patterns. Give the child a pencil, a brush, a sponge and a piece of chalk. Ask for a straight line, a wavy line, a zigzag and lots of dots with each one. Which tool made the thickest mark?
  • My journal. Keep a scrapbook. Stick in what was made this week, and ask: what did you make first? What would you do differently next time?
  • Kitchen patterns. You need: Spoons, forks, cups, or anything you have lots of. Put them in a row: spoon, fork, spoon, fork. Ask a grown-up what comes next. Now let the grown-up make a pattern, and YOU say what comes next. Look for: How many things did your pattern use? Two, or three?
  • Shape hunt. You need: A grown-up and a room. Find three circles, three squares and three rectangles. Find one triangle. They are the hardest! Draw the shapes you found. Look for: Which shape was easiest to find? Which was hardest?
  • A printed pattern. You need: A potato cut in half, paint on a plate, and a long strip of paper. Ask a grown-up to cut a shape into the flat side of the potato. Dip it in paint and print it on the paper. Print it again and again, all along the strip, turning it or changing colour to make a pattern. Look for: Say your pattern out loud. Does it repeat?

Answer keys

  • Shapes all around Which shape has three pointy corners? → triangle
  • Which shape is it? Circle: a clock, a ball; Square: a dice, a window pane; Triangle: a slice of pizza, a warning sign; Rectangle: a door, a phone
  • What comes next?
    • Red, blue, red, blue. What comes next? → red, blue
    • Yellow, yellow, green. What comes next? → yellow, yellow, green
    • Red, yellow, blue. What comes next? → red, yellow, blue
    then the child's own pattern: any row that repeats a unit of two or three tiles counts
  • Patterns in tiles find: Every tile has a star in the middle. The same shape, again and again.; A blue tile with a white star. Next to it is the opposite.; A white tile with a blue star. Blue, white, blue, white, across the whole wall.. How does the tile maker cover the whole wall? → with the same shapes, repeated again and again
  • Draw shapes and patterns the page judges the stroke itself: a circle with the crayon; a zigzag pattern with the pencil; a row of dots with the finger. Then a free drawing: no key
  • Shape and pattern quiz
    1. Red, blue, red, blue, red… what comes next? → blue
    2. A pattern is something that… → repeats
    3. Which shape is a squashed circle? → oval
    4. Which shape has four sides, two long and two short? → rectangle
  • My journal reads the page's own record of what the child made - the order they made them in IS the key; what they would change has none. If the making steps were skipped: made a pattern that repeats; made a circle with the crayon; made a zigzag pattern with the pencil; made a row of dots with the finger
  • Show what you know
    1. What is a shape? → the outside edge of a thing
    2. Why is blue, white, blue, white a pattern? → because the same colours come back in the same order
    3. A slice of pizza is a… → triangle
    4. Yellow, yellow, green, yellow, yellow… what comes next? → green
    5. Does a row of the same tile count as a pattern? → no, a pattern needs two or more different tiles that repeat
    6. What repeats on the tile wall? → a star shape
    7. To draw a circle, the line goes… → all the way round, back to the start
    8. Why do tile makers use the same shape on every tile? → so the tiles fit together and make a pattern

Where this art comes from

  • The cave wall is drawn in the manner of the painted caves of Europe, such as Lascaux in France (about 17,000 years ago) and Chauvet in France (more than 30,000 years ago), and of the hand stencils at the Cueva de las Manos in Argentina (about 9,000 years ago) and in the caves of Sulawesi in Indonesia, where some are more than 40,000 years old. People in many parts of the world made pictures like these long before writing. The picture on the page is our own drawing in that manner, not a copy of one wall.
  • Baskets are woven on every inhabited continent, from grass, reed, palm leaves, willow and pine needles. The basket on the page stands for that whole family of making, not for one people.
  • The striped cloth is drawn in the manner of kente, the woven cloth of Ghana, made by Asante and Ewe weavers in narrow strips that are sewn together. In real kente the patterns and colours have names and meanings that the weavers give them, so the page's stripes are our own simple pattern and not a real kente design. A photograph of a real one is well worth finding together.
  • The mask is drawn in the manner of the carved wooden face masks of West and Central Africa; the masks of the Dan people of Liberia and Côte d'Ivoire are one well-known example. Masks like these are made to be worn in dances and ceremonies and to tell stories, and what a mask means belongs to the people who make and wear it. So the lesson talks about what the carver did (the lines cut in, the dots painted on, the shape) and not about what a mask is for in any one community. The picture is our own drawing, not a copy of a real mask.
  • The tiled wall is drawn in the manner of the blue-and-white glazed tiles of Portugal (azulejos) and of İznik in Türkiye. Repeating star patterns are also at the heart of tile work across North Africa, the Middle East and Central Asia, such as the zellige of Morocco.
  • The dot painting is drawn in the manner of Aboriginal Australian painting from the Western Desert, where painting in dots on board and canvas began at Papunya in the early 1970s. Many of those paintings tell stories of Country, the land, that belong to particular families and places, and some designs may only be painted by the people they belong to. That is why the page draws its own rings and lines of dots rather than copying a real painting, and why the child is asked to make dots of their own in response, not to copy.

    2. What did the sister picture and the flowers BOTH have? → yellow
    3. A kind word about the basket should be about… → the weaving
    4. Before a show, artists… → make each piece a little better
  • Show what you know
    1. Red and blue make… → purple
    2. Texture is how a thing… → feels
    3. A pattern is something that… → repeats
    4. Wet clay joins… → wet clay
    5. Why keep a journal of your art? → to look back and see what you learned
    6. A kind comment is about… → something really in the picture
    7. Why change just one thing at a time? → so you can see what that change did
    8. Which shape has three corners? → a triangle
