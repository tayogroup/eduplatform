Step 1

A square number makes a square

Take a number, and lay out that many rows of that many dots. If you get a square, the total is a SQUARE NUMBER. 4 rows of 4 dots is 16, so 16 is a square number, and we write it 4², read "four squared". Slide the side of the square and watch the total.

16= 4²

The first twelve square numbers

The little 2 means "times itself". 4² is 4 × 4 = 16, not 4 × 2. Say it "four squared", because it is the number of dots in a four-by-four square. Every square number is a number multiplied by itself.
Step 2

Growing squares: add the next odd number

To turn a 3-by-3 square into a 4-by-4 square you add an L shape down one side and along the bottom. That is 3 + 3 + 1 = 7 new dots. Every L is an odd number, and each one is 2 bigger than the last. So the square numbers go 1, 4, 9, 16, 25 and the gaps between them go 3, 5, 7, 9. Slide to grow the square and watch the L.

The sequence so far

Odd numbers build squares. 1 + 3 = 4, then + 5 = 9, then + 7 = 16, then + 9 = 25. Add the first five odd numbers and you get 5². That is why the gaps between square numbers keep growing: this is a sequence, but NOT one with a "same step every time" rule.
Step 3

Is it a square number?

Given a number of dots, try to arrange them into a full square. If they fit exactly, it is a square number. If some are left over, it sits BETWEEN two square numbers. Pick a number and see the biggest square you can make.

Step 4

Square roots: the question the other way round

Step 1 asked "what is 7 squared?" and the answer was 49. A SQUARE ROOT asks the opposite: here are 49 dots, how many go along each side of the square? The answer is 7, and it is written √49 = 7. The roots worth knowing by heart

Roots that are not whole numbers

A root undoes a square. 7² = 49 and √49 = 7, so squaring a number and then taking the square root brings you back to where you started. That is why knowing the squares from Step 1 by heart gives you the roots for free: read the list backwards. And a number that is not a square number has a root that is not a whole number. The best you can say is which two whole numbers it lies between.
Step 5

Cube numbers: a square, stacked

A square number is a square of dots. A CUBE number is a cube of blocks: 3 blocks long, 3 wide and 3 high is 3 × 3 × 3 = 27 blocks. We write that 3³ and say "three cubed". The teal top is one layer, and one layer is a square number. The cube is that many layers deep, so a cube number is a square number stacked. Slide the edge and count the blocks.

27= 3³

Squares and cubes side by side

The little 3 means three copies multiplied, not "times 3". 3³ is 3 × 3 × 3 = 27. Two wrong answers both land on 9. One stops at the square, 3 × 3. The other reads the little 3 as "add it three times", 3 + 3 + 3. Watch the two columns in the table pull apart. At an edge of 2 the cube is only 4 more than the square. By an edge of 6 it is 216 against the square's 36, six times as many. Adding one to the edge of a cube adds a whole new layer, plus a strip down two of its sides.
Step 6

Cube roots: how long is the edge?

Step 4 undid a square, and this undoes a cube. Here is a pile of blocks. If they build a solid cube with none left over and no gaps, how many go along each edge? That number is the CUBE ROOT, written ∛64 = 4. Pick an edge and the cube gets built. You can see whether it swallows the pile exactly, runs out early, or asks for more than you have.

The cube roots worth knowing

Roots that are not whole numbers

The same number has a different square root and cube root. √64 = 8, because 8 × 8 = 64. ∛64 = 4, because 4 × 4 × 4 = 64. The square root splits the number into two equal parts multiplied; the cube root splits it into three. And there are far fewer cubes to recognise than squares. Up to 216 there are fourteen square numbers and only six cube numbers. So a cube root is usually found by trying two or three edges rather than remembered.
Step 7

Triangular numbers: rows that grow by one

Put 1 dot on the top row, 2 on the next, 3 on the next, and you build a triangle. The number of dots is a TRIANGULAR number: 1, 3, 6, 10, 15. Adding all those rows up is slow, so there is a trick. Turn a second triangle upside down, push it against the first, and the two make a neat rectangle. Slide the rows, then press the second view to see it happen.

10dots in 4 rows

Every triangle, and what it makes with the one before

Two triangles make a rectangle, and two triangles in a row make a square. A triangle of 4 rows and an upside-down copy fit into a 4 by 5 rectangle of 20 dots. So one triangle is 20 ÷ 2 = 10. That is where rows × (rows + 1) ÷ 2 comes from. It works however many rows you have, without adding a single one of them up. The other pairing is a surprise. Add two triangular numbers that sit NEXT to each other in the list, 6 + 10, and you get 16, a square number. Every neighbouring pair does it, which is why the last column of the table is full of squares.
