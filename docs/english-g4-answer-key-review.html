Name three jobs Omar does at the post counter each morning.

answer: He sorts the mail, weighs the parcels, and helps people send letters.

The second sentence of the text lists all three morning jobs in order.

the text it is asked about
Omar runs the market shop, and the town post counter stands beside his till. Every morning he sorts the mail, weighs the parcels, and helps people send letters. Before the market grows busy, he lines up the parcels by size and checks each address twice, because a small mistake could send a letter to the wrong street. He also reads letters aloud for neighbours who find long words difficult, sitting patiently while Grandmother Salma or old Mr Hani listens for news from a cousin far away. “Not everyone owns a phone,” he says. “A letter can bring peace and make someone feel remembered.” Omar speaks two languages, so he can serve almost every customer in town, switching smoothly between them without ever losing his patient smile. His work is not fast, but he does it with effort and care. When the shelves look crowded, he simply works a little longer instead of leaving a job half finished. Children from the school sometimes stop by after class to watch him weigh a heavy parcel or to ask what a strange stamp means. Amal is one of them; she likes to watch how carefully he checks every name before a letter leaves the counter. “Some days are busy and some are quiet, but every day matters,” Omar says, and he always closes the counter only after the last customer has gone home happy.

“I think so,” said Amal. “I will try my best and continue learning.”

“Of course,” he smiled. “That sounds like a good project.”
“What do you do in your daily work?” she asked.
“I sort the mail, help people send letters, and serve customers. I also read letters for people who find long words difficult.”

“Because not everyone owns a phone. A letter can bring peace, and it can make someone feel remembered.”
Amal asked whether he spoke more than one language. “Yes,” he said. “I had to master two languages to serve everyone here. It is necessary, so that nobody leaves the counter without help.”
“And do you do this job every day?” Amal asked.
“Yes,” he said. “It is my usual work. Some days are busy and some are quiet, but every day matters.”
Back home, Amal began her report. She wanted it to be fair and honest, so she used every detail. She checked her spelling and kept her handwriting neat.
Her father read over her shoulder. “Take your time,” he said gently. “Do not leave out anything important.”
“I won't,” she replied. “This report has a purpose.”

Afterwards a judge asked, “What did you learn from this?”
“I learned to listen, to write clearly, and to keep going,” Amal said with a smile.
Later that day Teacher Yasmin made the announcement. “The winner is Amal! Her report was honest, clear and full of meaning.” The whole class clapped, and Amal stood proudly with her prize, a brand-new dictionary.
At home her mother hugged her. “You did it!”
“Yes,” said Amal. “I did not stop. I worked hard, and I found a good balance.” She opened her dictionary and pointed to one word. “Look: effort means work done with care. Now I really know what it means.”

the text it is asked about
Weather is different all around the world. In some places, the air is foggy and grey in the morning, and people walk slowly along the path until the mist begins to lift. In other places, a hot sun makes people sweat, especially where dry canyons stretch for miles under a clear sky. Near a quiet bay or a green meadow, the weather often feels gentler, but even calm places can change quickly once the wind turns.
High in the mountains, snow falls softly and covers the ground in white, while down by the coast, moisture from the sea keeps the air feeling damp all day. Rain helps plants grow, because plants need moisture, so farmers watch the clouds and hope for a steady shower rather than a flood.
Not all weather is gentle. A gentle storm brings rain, but a hurricane brings very strong wind that can bend trees and tear roofs from houses. Part 1: Choose your six pages. Take them from at least five different units. Your set must show reading, writing, speaking and new words.

Part 2: Build your display board. Write two paragraphs on every page. Add one picture and one clear label to each page. Print your name and your class on the front.

Part 3: Prepare your talk. It lasts two minutes. Open with your name, present three pages, then answer two questions from the audience.

Part 4: Write your reflection. Name one thing that was genuinely hard this year, one thing that improved, and one goal for Grade 5.

Deadlines. Pages chosen by Tuesday. Board finished by Thursday. Talk rehearsed with a partner by Thursday evening. The Exhibition opens on Friday at five o'clock in the school hall.

Marks. Sixteen marks for the board, sixteen for the talk, sixteen for your corrected draft, and sixteen for your reflection.

One rule matters more than the rest: every page must be your own work, written in your own English voice.
