Step 8

Sequences with a rule: start, then keep doing it

A sequence is a list of numbers in order, and a term is one number in the list. A rule like "start at 5 and add 4" makes the whole sequence: each term comes from the one before it. Pick a start number and a rule, then read the terms off the strip. The arrows show the rule being used again and again.

The rule

Working

Two things make a sequence: where it starts and the rule. "Add 4" starting at 5 gives 5, 9, 13, 17. The same rule starting at 3 gives 3, 7, 11, 15: different sequence, same rule. And the rule is used one time FEWER than the number of terms: to reach the 6th term you use it 5 times.
Step 9

Find the rule from the terms

Now the sequence is given and the rule is hidden. Look at the GAP between one term and the next. If the gap is the same every time, the rule is "add that" or "subtract that". If the gaps change, look closer: maybe each term is double the last, or maybe the gaps are the odd numbers from Step 2. Pick a sequence and reveal it one move at a time.

Pick a sequence

Working

Check the rule on EVERY gap, not just the first. 2, 4, 6 looks like "add 2" and 2, 4, 8 looks like "add 2" for one gap. But the next gap is 4, so the rule there is "double". One gap can fool you; two or three gaps cannot.
Step 10

Two shapes, two facts

In the last two steps you knew the rule and worked out the terms. Now you know two facts and have to work out two numbers. A shape standing in for a number nobody has told you yet is where algebra begins. Two facts about two shapes are always exactly enough. One would leave you guessing and three could contradict each other.

They add to … and differ by …

All six, side by side

Adding the two facts is the trick, and it works because one shape cancels itself out. Put ▲ + ● = 10 underneath ▲ − ● = 4 and add them. The ● is added once and taken away once, so it disappears entirely. That leaves ▲ + ▲ = 14. That is one shape on its own, and halving it finishes the job. Subtracting the facts instead makes the ▲ disappear the same way. Two facts, two unknowns. One fact on its own leaves a whole list of answers. "They add to 10" is true of 9 and 1, 8 and 2, 7 and 3 and more. The second fact is what cuts the list down to one. And the shapes are only placeholders. A letter would do exactly the same job, which is what secondary school does. Write x and y instead of ▲ and ● and nothing else about the method changes.
Step 11

Squares and cubes hiding in a sequence

Step 8 said to look at the gaps, and that if they are all the same the rule is "add that". So what do you do when the gaps are NOT the same? Look at the gaps of the gaps. Squares and cubes turn up as sequences all the time, and this is how you catch them. Pick a sequence and say what is hiding in it.

Pick a sequence

What is hiding in it?

The gaps, and the gaps of those

Working

The gaps of the gaps are the giveaway. Every square-number sequence settles on the same one: 2, every single time. That holds whether the squares climb from 1², starting further along at 3², sitting one above a square, or counting back down from 10². Cube numbers never settle that quickly, because a cube climbs faster than a square: their gaps of gaps are 12, 18, 24 and still growing. And a plain "add 4" sequence has gaps of gaps of 0, because its gaps never change at all.
Step 12

Shape patterns: count, then find the rule

Patterns made of matchsticks or dots grow by a rule too. Count how many pieces pattern 1, pattern 2 and pattern 3 use, write the counts as a sequence, and the gap tells you the rule. Then you can say how many pieces pattern 10 needs without building it. Pick a pattern and slide through it.

Pick a pattern

Working

The gap is what one more piece of the pattern costs. One more square on the end of a row of matchstick squares needs 3 sticks, not 4, because it shares a side. That is why the counts go up in 3s. Look at the picture to see WHY the rule is what it is, and the sequence to see WHAT it is.
Step 13

Steps on a number line: decimals, and below zero

A rule does not have to be a whole number, and a sequence does not have to stop at zero. "Start at 2, subtract 0.5" gives 2, 1.5, 1, 0.5, 0, −0.5. Watch the hops on the line: every hop is the same size, and the line simply carries on past zero into the negative numbers. Pick a start and a step.

The step

Working

Going down past zero. 1, then 1 − 3 is −2, not 2. On the line, subtracting means hopping LEFT, and the numbers left of zero are negative: −1, −2, −3. The hops stay the same size, so the pattern does not change just because the sign did.
